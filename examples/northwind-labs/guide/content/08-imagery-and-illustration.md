# Photography and illustration

Our images show what climate data is for: real cities, real infrastructure and the people who run them. They should feel as confident, precise and optimistic as our logo. They should be clear, well composed and hopeful without being naive.

## Photography style

### Subjects

- **Cities and infrastructure:** streets, rooftops, tree canopy, transit, substations, water treatment plants, wind and solar installations, flood defences.
- **People at work:** sustainability officers, engineers and field teams using data, in control rooms, on site or in meetings.
- **Climate in context:** heat, rain and wind shown through their effect on places, such as a shaded street or a drainage channel after rain. Never show disaster or suffering for effect.
- **Our work:** screens and printouts of our maps and dashboards in real use, with real (or approved sample) data.

### Composition

- Use clear structure: strong lines, horizons and repeating forms, echoing the geometry of our logo.
- Look up. Low angles and rising lines (towers, turbines, stairs, a horizon at dawn) echo the upward peak in our symbol.
- Leave calm space, such as open sky, water or a plain wall, where text or the logo can sit.
- Use one clear subject per image.

### Light and colour

- Natural, clean light: early morning and clear daylight are ideal. The warm light of daybreak is a natural link to Daybreak Amber.
- Grade images gently towards cool teal shadows and clean, neutral highlights. Keep skin tones, vegetation and sky natural.
- Avoid heavy filters, crushed blacks, oversaturation and strong vignettes.

## People and representation

Our audiences and the communities they serve are diverse. Our images must show that honestly.

- Across a set of images, include a range of ages, ethnicities, genders, body types and disabilities.
- Show disabled people as active participants and decision-makers, such as an engineer who uses a wheelchair leading a site visit, not only as people being helped.
- Show women and under-represented groups in technical and senior roles, as a matter of course rather than as a statement.
- Avoid tokenism: one diverse face in an otherwise uniform set is not representation.
- Photograph real places, with permission, rather than generic skylines that could be anywhere.

## What to avoid

- Staged stock clichés: handshakes, people pointing at blank screens, glowing globes, a green leaf in a hand, light bulbs with plants inside.
- Doom imagery: burning forests or flooded homes used for shock.
- Text, logos or numbers baked into photographs. Add text as live text so it can be translated, read by screen readers and kept accurate.
- Fake data on screens that could be mistaken for real results.

## Text over images

Text placed directly on photographs is hard to read, because brightness changes across the image.

- Prefer placing text on a solid panel in a brand colour next to or over the image.
- If text must sit on the image, add a scrim, a gradient overlay in Polar Night, under the text area.
- Check contrast at the **lightest point** behind the text, not the average. Text must reach 4.5:1 (or 3:1 for large text) at that point.
- Apply the logo rules in chapter 3: the logo goes only on a calm area or a solid panel.

## Illustration

Use illustration to explain things that photographs cannot, such as how a sensor network works, how heat moves through a city, or a process.

- **Style:** flat, geometric and diagrammatic. Build from circles, triangles and straight lines, like the logo.
- **Line weight:** 2px at the 24px icon scale, scaled in proportion, with sharp corners to match our icons.
- **Palette:** use the brand scales only. Neutrals and Fjord Teal carry most of the drawing; use Daybreak Amber for one focal detail; use Polar Night for structure and outlines.
- **Detail:** only the detail needed to explain the idea. No textures, gradients or 3D shading.
- **People:** simplified, with varied skin tones and body types, never using stereotype shorthand.
- **Meaningful lines and shapes** must reach 3:1 against their background.

## Sourcing and rights

- Use our own commissioned photography first, then licensed stock from an approved library.
- Keep a record of every image's source, licence, expiry date, usage limits and credit line.
- Get signed model releases for identifiable people, and property releases for private buildings where required.
- Credit photographers where the licence requires it, in the format in chapter 20.
- Never take images from search engines or social media without written permission.

## Alt text

Every meaningful image needs alt text. Write it for the purpose of the image, not its appearance.

- Say what the image tells the reader in this context, in about 125 characters or fewer.
- Do not start with "Image of" or "Photo of".
- Decorative images, such as a texture or a repeat of nearby text, get empty alt text so screen readers skip them.
- For images containing data, see chapter 9 for chart and map alt text.

Example: "Utility engineer checking flood sensor readings on a tablet beside a storm drain", not "Woman with tablet".

## AI-generated imagery

- **Disclose it.** Label AI-generated or AI-altered images in the caption or credits, and follow each platform's labelling rules.
- **No real people** in realistic AI imagery without their written consent, and never public officials or customers.
- **No fake evidence.** Never use AI imagery to depict real places, events, climate impacts, infrastructure or data as if they were photographs.
- **Review every image** for bias, stereotypes and artefacts, such as distorted hands, wrong text or impossible structures, before use.
- **Check the terms** of the tool for commercial use and ownership. If in doubt, ask the Marketing team.

## Imagery: do and don't
> **Do** show real people and real places using climate data with confidence.

> **Do** check text contrast at the lightest point of the image.

> **Do** describe the purpose of each image in its alt text.

> **Don't** use disaster imagery for shock value.

> **Don't** bake text or numbers into photographs.

> **Don't** present AI-generated images as real places, events or data.
