# Voice and tone

Our voice is who we are in words, and it stays the same everywhere. Our tone is how we adjust that voice to the moment: a product error, a council report and an investor update all sound like Northwind Labs, but they do not sound identical. Climate data is only useful if people trust it, and people trust organisations that are clear about what they know, what they do not know and what to do next.

Our voice must work for two kinds of reader at once: technical readers, such as utility engineers, who need exact units, methods and definitions; and policy and investor readers, who need the finding, the confidence level and the decision it supports. When in doubt, lead with the plain-language finding and put the technical detail directly underneath it.

## Voice attributes

The first three attributes come from the personality the organisation chose (see chapter 2). *Proposed — confirm with leadership:* the fourth, Clear, holds the other three together and carries our accessibility commitment.

### Confident

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Direct. We lead with the finding and stand behind our methods. | Arrogant, dismissive of other approaches, or certain where the data is not. |
| Calm and specific when things go wrong. | Defensive, vague or evasive. |

Example: "Peak demand on this feeder is likely to exceed its rating within five years. Here is the evidence and what you can do about it."

### Precise

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Exact about numbers, units, dates, sources and uncertainty. | Pedantic, or so caveated that the point disappears. |
| Careful to say "estimated", "modelled" or "measured", and to mean it. | Loose with words like "proven", "guaranteed" or "zero". |

Example: "We estimate the programme avoided 1,800 to 2,300 tCO₂e in 2025 (central estimate 2,050 tCO₂e). The methodology note explains how."

### Optimistic

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Focused on progress and on the practical steps that are possible now. | Hyped, utopian, or promising to "solve" climate change. |
| Honest about risk, and constructive about what to do with it. | Doom-laden, alarmist or fear-driven. |

Example: "The gap to your 2030 target is real, and it is closing. These three measures would make the biggest difference."

### Clear

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Plain, structured and easy to scan, with the most important point first. | Jargon-heavy, acronym-heavy, or written only for specialists. |
| Respectful of every reader's time, expertise and ability. | Patronising or over-simplified to the point of being wrong. |

Example: "This map shows which streets are most likely to flood in heavy rain. Darker areas and the hatched pattern show higher risk."

> **Do** state the finding, the confidence and the next step, in that order.

> **Don't** borrow hype from the wider tech industry. Words like "revolutionary", "game-changing" and "AI-powered magic" undermine the precision people buy from us.

## Tone by context

Use the dial to set tone. Formality runs from 1 (conversational) to 5 (formal). Seriousness runs from 1 (light) to 5 (serious). Our voice never goes below 2 on either dial in external content: we are never flippant about climate impacts, people's jobs or public money.

| Context | Formality | Seriousness | Example | Words and habits to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marketing and campaigns | 2 | 3 | "Know where your grid will be under pressure, before it is." | Superlatives, "world-leading", "solve climate change", unsupported numbers |
| Website | 3 | 3 | "We help utilities see where demand is growing and why." | Jargon in headings, vague benefit claims, "click here" |
| Product and UI microcopy | 2 | 2 | Button: "Export as CSV". Empty state: "No sites yet. Add a site to see its emissions profile." | "Oops", "Whoops", blame, humour in data views |
| Customer support | 2 | 3 | "Thanks for flagging this. You are right that the totals do not match. We are checking the source data and will reply by 4pm today." | "Unfortunately", "as per my last email", "user error" |
| Error messages | 2 | 3 | "We could not load the 2024 data. Check your connection and try again. Your saved work is safe." | "Fatal", "illegal", "invalid user", codes without explanation |
| Data reports and methodology | 4 | 4 | "Emissions fell by an estimated 6% (range 4% to 8%) compared with 2024, mainly because of lower grid carbon intensity." | "Proves", "guarantees", false precision, unexplained acronyms |
| Bad news and crisis | 4 | 5 | "On 2 October 2026 we found an error in our heat-risk model that affected reports issued since June. We have corrected it and are contacting every affected customer today." | Minimising, passive voice that hides responsibility, speculation, humour |
| Social posts | 2 | 3 | "Heatwaves put pressure on city grids. Our new guide explains how to plan for peak demand. Link in comments." | Hashtag stuffing, emoji in the middle of sentences, engagement bait |
| Social replies and complaints | 2 | 4 | "Sorry this has been frustrating. We would like to look into it. Please email hello@northwindlabs.example and we will reply within one working day." | Arguing in public, copy-paste replies, sharing private details |
| Recruitment | 2 | 3 | "You will build models that city teams use to plan real infrastructure." | "Rockstar", "ninja", "young and dynamic", gendered terms |
| Investor relations | 4 | 4 | "Revenue for the half-year was £[X], up [Y]% on the same period last year. The main risks are set out on page [N]." | Forward-looking claims without caveats, impact claims without evidence, inconsistent figures |
| Legal and contracts | 5 | 4 | Plain English, defined terms and short clauses. | Unnecessary Latin, "herein", "hereinafter", hidden key terms |
| Internal communications | 2 | 2 | "Quick update: the new data pipeline is live. Tell us in the team channel if anything looks wrong." | In-jokes that exclude new staff, acronyms only one team knows |

> **Accessibility** Error messages must say what happened, why if known, and what to do next, and must be announced to assistive technology. Never rely on red text or an icon alone to show that something went wrong.

## Talking about numbers and uncertainty

Our audiences make decisions with our numbers, so how we present certainty matters as much as the number itself.

- **Say how we know.** Use "measured", "modelled", "estimated" or "forecast" deliberately. They mean different things.
- **Give a range when we have one.** "An estimated 1,800 to 2,300 tCO₂e" is more honest and more useful than "2,047 tCO₂e".
- **Match precision to confidence.** Do not report more significant figures than the data supports. Round, and say you have rounded.
- **Name the baseline and period.** "Compared with 2019" or "between April and September 2026", never just "a 20% reduction".
- **Use calibrated words consistently.** Reserve "very likely" for strong evidence and "may" for weak evidence. Chapter 12 sets the exact terms.
- **Explain technical units once, in plain words.** "Tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (tCO₂e), a single measure that combines different greenhouse gases."

## Environmental claims and greenwashing

Greenwashing damages trust in us and in our customers. Our audiences include regulators' readers, investors and journalists, so treat every environmental claim as one that may be checked.

In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority's (CMA) Green Claims Code sets out six principles for environmental claims. Claims must:

1. be truthful and accurate
2. be clear and unambiguous
3. not omit or hide important relevant information
4. make only fair and meaningful comparisons
5. consider the full life cycle of the product or service
6. be substantiated

Advertising claims must also follow the Advertising Standards Authority's rules. Investors and partners may have their own disclosure rules.

How we apply this:

- **Every impact claim needs evidence on file**: the data, method, period, scope and who checked it. If you cannot point to it, do not publish the claim.
- **Separate our impact from our customers' impact.** We provide analysis; a city or utility takes the action. Write "helped [customer] identify" rather than "we cut emissions by", unless the customer has approved the wording and the evidence supports it.
- **Avoid absolute claims** such as "carbon neutral", "zero emissions", "green" or "sustainable" without a defined scope, standard and evidence.
- **Be careful with equivalences.** Comparisons such as "the same as taking 500 cars off the road" must come from a cited, recognised source and use the same scope and period.
- **Get customer approval** before naming a customer or quoting their results.

> **Warning** This section is editorial guidance, not legal advice. Ask a qualified adviser to review high-profile environmental claims, investor communications and anything published in a new country.

## Before and after

These rewrites use typical Northwind Labs situations. The figures are illustrative.

| Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Our revolutionary AI platform will slash your city's emissions." | "Our analysis shows where emission cuts are most achievable, so you can focus budget where it counts." | Removes hype and an outcome we do not control; describes what we do. |
| "Emissions were reduced by 23.47% thanks to Northwind." | "Emissions fell by an estimated 21% to 26% compared with 2022. Northwind Labs analysis helped the council prioritise the measures behind this." | Matches precision to confidence, names the baseline and credits the customer's action. |
| "Error 503: Data ingestion failure." | "We could not import your meter data. The file may be too large. Try splitting it into files under 50 MB, or contact support." | Says what happened, a likely cause and what to do next. |
| "Utilising our proprietary methodology, stakeholders can leverage actionable insights." | "City teams and engineers can use our results to decide what to do next." | Plain words, a real audience and a real action. |
| "It's too late to stop climate change, but we can help you survive it." | "Some climate impacts are already locked in. Planning now reduces their cost and keeps services running." | Optimistic and honest, without fear-driven framing. |
| "Unfortunately, due to circumstances beyond our control, some reports may have been impacted." | "An error in our model affected flood-risk scores in reports issued between June and September. We have fixed it, and we will send corrected reports by 9 October 2026." | Takes responsibility, gives specifics and a date. |
| "Check out the green button on the right to grab your free report!!! 🌍🔥" | "Download the free report on summer peak demand (PDF, 2 MB)." | Descriptive link, no directional cue, no emoji in a call to action. |

## Words we use and words we avoid

| Use | Avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|
| estimated, modelled, measured, forecast | proven, guaranteed, exact (for estimates) | Shows how we know |
| reduce, cut (with a number and baseline) | slash, eliminate, solve | Avoids overclaiming |
| help, use, show, explain | leverage, utilise, empower, unlock | Plain language |
| city teams, engineers, residents | stakeholders (on its own), end users, the public (as a mass) | Names real people |
| climate risk, climate impacts | climate apocalypse, catastrophe (unless quoting) | Serious without alarmism |
| net zero, decarbonisation (with a defined target) | carbon neutral, eco-friendly, green (without evidence) | Green Claims Code |
| data shows, our analysis suggests | data proves, science says | Honest about evidence |
| gap, area we have not covered | blind spot | Avoids ableist metaphor |
| sample data, test data | dummy data | Avoids ableist metaphor |
| primary and replica, allowlist and blocklist | master and slave, whitelist and blacklist | Inclusive technical terms |
| person-hours, staffing | man-hours, manpower | Gender-neutral |

> **Do** read your draft aloud. If it sounds like a press release from an unnamed tech start-up, rewrite it until it sounds like a knowledgeable colleague.

> **Don't** use fear, guilt or urgency without evidence to drive action. Our optimism is the reason people want to work with us.
