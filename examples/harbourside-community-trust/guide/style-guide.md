## Introduction

Harbourside Community Trust exists to help families in Harbourside's coastal neighbourhoods find safe homes, warm meals and good work. People trust us when everything we make looks, sounds and feels the same: a poster in a community centre, a WhatsApp message to a volunteer, a letter to a family or a report for the council. This guide shows you how to make that happen.

### Who this guide is for

This guide is for everyone who makes something with our name on it:

- **Staff and volunteers** writing posts, posters, letters, emails and signs.
- **The Communications team**, who look after the brand day to day.
- **Designers, printers, web developers and agencies** we work with.
- **Partners and funders**, including the council, who show our logo next to theirs.

You do not need to be a designer to use it. Most chapters start with the reason for a rule, then give the rule, then show a **Do** and a **Don't**.

### How to use this guide

1. **Start with the basics.** Read *Brand foundations*, *Logo*, *Colour*, *Typography* and *Voice and tone*. They cover most everyday work.
2. **Go to the chapter for your job.** Making a social post? Go to *Social media*. Writing a letter to families? Go to *Writing style* and *Presentations and documents*.
3. **Use the ready-made files.** Logos, social templates, favicons and design tokens are in the asset kit listed in *Legal, governance and assets*. Starting from these files is faster and keeps you on-brand.
4. **Check accessibility before you publish.** Use the checklist in *Accessibility*.

### What is fixed and what is flexible

| Fixed: always follow | Flexible: use your judgement |
|---|---|
| The logo artwork, its colours, clear space and minimum size | Which approved logo version suits the background |
| Brand colour values and the approved colour pairings | How much of each brand colour a layout uses, within the proportions |
| Accessibility rules: contrast, text size, alt text, captions | Photography subjects and crops, within the imagery rules |
| Our name, written as *Harbourside Community Trust* | Tone, which shifts with the situation (see *Voice and tone*) |
| Consent rules for photos and stories about people we help | Layout, as long as it uses the grid and spacing scale |

If a rule stops you doing something sensible, talk to the Communications team before you break it. We would rather improve the guide than have people work around it.

### Our accessibility commitment

Many of the people we serve are older, live with a disability, or speak English as a second language. So accessibility is not an extra in this guide: it is built into every rule. Everything we publish must meet WCAG 2.2 level AA as a minimum. That means readable text sizes, strong colour contrast, plain English, alt text on images, captions on video, and an offline or phone route to help for anyone who cannot use digital channels. The *Accessibility* chapter explains how.

### Getting help

Questions about the brand, requests for logo files, or approval of a new design go to the Communications team at [comms@harbourside.example](mailto:comms@harbourside.example). Please allow at least five working days for approvals; *Legal, governance and assets* sets out the approval process.

> **Do** start from the templates and logo files in the asset kit.

> **Don't** redraw, retype or recolour the logo, or copy it from a website or a screenshot.

> **Note** Parts of this guide are proposals, such as our vision, tagline, colour names and fonts. They are marked as proposals until our leadership confirms them.

## Brand foundations

Everything we design or write starts here. When you know what we do, who we do it for and how we behave, you can make good choices in any situation this guide does not cover.

Some parts of this chapter were given to us by Harbourside Community Trust: the mission and the four value names. Everything else is a proposal from the brand team and is marked *Proposed — confirm with leadership*. Do not publish proposed wording outside the organisation until leadership has approved it.

### Mission

The mission says what we do, for whom and why. Use it word for word.

> We help families in Harbourside's coastal neighbourhoods find safe homes, warm meals and good work.

Three ideas sit inside the mission. Keep them in this order and in these words when you summarise our work:

- **Safe homes**
- **Warm meals**
- **Good work**

> **Don't** add services, numbers or results to the mission. If you need to describe a specific service, check the facts with the Communications team first.

### Vision

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* "A Harbourside where every family has a safe home, enough to eat and work they can be proud of."

The vision is the future we are working towards. It is not a promise or a claim about results. Use it in strategy documents, annual reviews and talks, not in short social posts.

### Positioning

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* "For families in Harbourside's coastal neighbourhoods who need help with a home, food or work, Harbourside Community Trust is the local charity that helps them find all three in one place, because we are part of the community we serve and we work alongside neighbours, volunteers and partners."

The positioning is for internal use. It helps you decide what to say first. It is not a public slogan.

> **Note** "In one place" and "part of the community" are claims. Leadership must confirm both are true before we use them in public copy.

### Values

The four value names come from the organisation. Use them exactly as written, with a capital letter, in this order. The descriptions and behaviours are proposals.

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* descriptions and behaviours for each value.

| Value | What it means | How it shows in our words |
|---|---|---|
| **Kindness** | We treat everyone with warmth, patience and respect. | We write as a friendly neighbour would speak: warm, calm and never judging. |
| **Fairness** | Everyone deserves the same chance and the same respect. | We describe people with dignity, explain rules clearly and never hide the small print. |
| **Together** | We do more when families, volunteers, donors and partners work side by side. | We say "with", not "for". We credit volunteers and partners by name when they agree. |
| **Local first** | We belong to Harbourside and put local people and places first. | We name real streets, places and local partners, and we write for the people who live here. |

> **Do** show a value through behaviour: "Our volunteers will meet you at the door and help you fill in the form."

> **Don't** list our values as adjectives in a sentence such as "We are kind, fair and local." Show them instead.

### Brand personality

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* our personality comes from the logo: a rising sun over calm waves, rounded letters and warm colour. If Harbourside Community Trust were a person, they would be:

| Trait | What it means | Links to voice attribute (chapter 11) |
|---|---|---|
| **Warm** | Friendly and kind, like a neighbour at the door | Warm, not sentimental |
| **Steady** | Calm and reliable, even when things are hard | Calm and steady, not cold |
| **Approachable** | Easy to talk to; no jargon, no barriers | Clear and simple, not childish |
| **Hopeful** | Looks towards better days, like the rising sun | Hopeful and honest, not over-promising |
| **Rooted** | Proud to be from Harbourside and the coast | Rooted in Harbourside, not parochial |

We are not corporate, and we are not playful in a childish way. We are the steady, friendly people you are glad to see.

### Audiences

Our audiences were supplied by the organisation. Their needs and channels below are *Proposed — confirm with leadership*, based on the audience list and the platforms we use (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, LinkedIn and our website).

Many of our readers are older residents, and many speak English as a second language. Write every message so that both groups can understand it the first time they read it.

| Audience | What they need from us | Main channels | Plan for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Local families** | Clear, kind information about what help is available, who can get it, where to go and what to bring. Respect and privacy. | WhatsApp, Facebook, website, printed leaflets and posters | Plain English, short sentences, translations, Easy Read versions, information that works on a basic phone and offline |
| **Volunteers** | What to do, when and where; how their time helps; thanks and recognition. | WhatsApp, Facebook, email, website | Clear shift details, simple sign-up steps, mixed ages and levels of digital confidence |
| **Donors** | Honest information about where their money goes, and simple ways to give. | Website, Facebook, Instagram, email, LinkedIn | No guilt or pressure, clear consent choices, accessible donation forms |
| **Council partners** | Reliable facts, clear contacts and evidence of local need, presented professionally. | Email, LinkedIn, reports and documents | Accessible documents (tagged PDF or Word), defined terms, no unsupported claims |
| **Older residents** | Large, clear text, simple steps and a phone number or place to visit. | Printed materials, phone, Facebook, face to face | At least 16px on screen and 14pt in print, no "online only" routes, no small grey text |
| **People who speak English as a second language** | Short, literal sentences they can read or translate easily. | WhatsApp, Facebook, printed materials | No idioms, consistent words, translated versions where possible, simple icons with text |

> **Accessibility** Always give at least one way to get help that does not need the internet, such as a phone number or an address with opening times.

### Tagline

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* **"Here for Harbourside."**

It is short, literal and easy to translate. It works next to the logo, at the end of a video and as a sign-off on social posts.

Use the tagline in sentence case with a full stop when it stands alone. Never change its words or add a second line to it.

## Logo

Our logo is the quickest way people recognise us: on a food bank poster, a WhatsApp message from a volunteer or a council partner's report. Using it the same way every time builds trust with families who may be meeting us at a hard moment, so treat it as fixed artwork and follow these rules.

### Our logo

The logo is a **combination mark**: a circular symbol on the left and the name, set as a wordmark, on the right.

The symbol shows a Sunset Coral half-sun rising over three white wave lines inside a solid Harbour Blue circle, with a Tide Blue band between the lower waves. It describes where we work: Harbourside's coastal neighbourhoods. The rising sun is warm and hopeful, and the waves are calm rather than stormy. The heavy, rounded letters of "Harbourside" feel friendly and steady, not corporate.

Those cues of round shapes, soft curves, warm colour and heavy, approachable letters run through the whole identity. They are why our corners are rounded (chapter 06), our icons have rounded ends (chapter 07) and our display typeface has soft terminals (chapter 05).

### Anatomy

| Part | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Symbol | The Harbour Blue circle containing the sun, the waves and the Tide Blue band | Can be used on its own in square and circular spaces |
| Wordmark, line 1 | "Harbourside" in a heavy rounded sans-serif, Harbour Blue | The dominant part of the name |
| Wordmark, line 2 | "Community Trust" in a lighter weight, Sunset Coral | Smallest detail in the logo: this sets the minimum size |
| Lock-up | The fixed size and spacing between symbol and wordmark | Never change it |

The white wave lines break through the edge of the circle. That is part of the drawing, not a printing fault, so never "tidy" it by redrawing the circle.

**Why knockout.** Our single-colour logos use the *knockout* style: the sun and the waves are cut out of the circle so the background shows through. The plain single-colour style fills the whole circle with one colour and erases the sun and waves completely, so we never use it. In knockout, the Tide Blue band merges into the circle, leaving two clear waves. That is expected.

The variant files were produced automatically from a PNG. Before you use a single-colour logo for print, signage, embroidery or engraving, ask the Communications team for designer-checked vector artwork.

### Clear space

Keep a clear area around the logo, free of text, images, edges and other logos. The rule, shown in the specifications table below, is based on the height of the sun in the symbol, so it scales with the logo and you can measure it on any proof by eye.

Clear space gives the logo room to be noticed and stops it being read as part of nearby text or a partner's logo. Treat the clear space as a minimum: more space is always fine. The `-clearspace` PNG files have it built in, so placing them edge to edge with other content keeps you safe.

> **Do** use the `-clearspace` versions of the logo files in Word, PowerPoint and Canva, so the margin comes with the file.

> **Don't** let the clear space overlap a page edge, a fold, a photo edge or a partner's logo.

### Minimum size

**Logo specifications**

| Rule | Specification |
|---|---|
| Logo type | combination |
| Clear space | Keep clear space on all sides equal to the height of the sun in the symbol (about a quarter of the logo's height). |
| Minimum size (screen) | 160 px wide |
| Minimum size (print) | 40 mm wide |
| Symbol minimum size | 24 px / 8 mm |
| Proportions | 3.471 : 1 (width : height), wide-horizontal |
| Alt text | “Harbourside Community Trust logo” |
The minimum size is set by "Community Trust", the smallest and lightest part of the logo. Below it, the letters blur on screen and fill in when printed on uncoated paper.

- **Below the full logo's minimum width**, switch to the symbol and write our name in text nearby (for example, in the post caption, the page title or the line under the icon).
- **Below the symbol's minimum size**, don't use the logo at all. Favicons are the only exception and have their own prepared files (chapter 14).
- **Signage and large print:** there is no maximum size, but you need vector artwork to print large (see "Files and naming").

### Placement

Place the logo consistently so people know where to look for it.

| Format | Position | Size guide |
|---|---|---|
| Website and email header | Top left, aligned to the grid's left margin, linked to the home page | Never below the minimum width; on mobile, keep the full logo if it fits, otherwise use the symbol with our name as text |
| Letters, reports, A4 and A5 print | Top left, inside the page margin | About a quarter to a third of the page width on covers; smaller on inside pages |
| Posters and flyers | Top left or bottom left, inside the safe margin | Large enough to read from the expected viewing distance |
| Social posts and video | Bottom left or bottom right, inside the platform's safe zone (chapter 15) | Same corner and size across a whole campaign |
| Slides | Title and end slides only; small in a footer corner on content slides if needed | Never on every slide at full size |
| Avatars and app icons | Symbol, centred | Leave the circular crop margin built into the asset files |

Align the logo to the layout grid (chapter 06): the left edge of the artwork, not of its clear space, sits on the margin. Use one placement for every item in a campaign.

#### How to choose

- **White:** use the full-colour logo. This is our master presentation.
- **Light neutral, Sunset Coral and Tide Blue:** use the single-colour dark knockout logo. Full colour is not allowed on these backgrounds: on light neutral, "Community Trust" in Sunset Coral drops further below 3:1, and on Sunset Coral or Tide Blue, parts of the logo disappear into the background.
- **Harbour Blue and dark neutral:** use the single-colour light (reversed) knockout logo. The Harbour Blue circle and wordmark would vanish on these backgrounds.
- **Tints and other colours:** use whichever single-colour version reaches at least 3:1 against the background. If in doubt, ask the Communications team.

**The white-background exception.** In full colour, "Community Trust" in Sunset Coral is 2.95:1 against white, just under the 3:1 we require for graphics. WCAG 2.2 exempts logotypes from contrast requirements, so the full-colour logo is approved on white as our master presentation. This exception is pending sign-off by the Communications team, and it has two conditions. First, the logo must never be the only place our name appears: the name must also be in live text, alt text or the page title. Second, the exception covers white only, not off-whites, tints or paper colours.

#### Photographs and patterns

- Place the logo only on a calm, even part of a photo, such as sky, sea or a plain wall, where the area directly behind the logo keeps at least 3:1 with it.
- If no calm area exists, put the logo on a solid white panel (full colour) or a Harbour Blue panel (reversed), with the clear space inside the panel. Use radius tokens from chapter 06 for the panel corners.
- Never place the logo over faces, text, busy patterns, or the join between light and dark areas.

### Misuse

The logo only works if it looks the same everywhere. Don't change it in any of these ways.

> **Don't** stretch, squash or skew the logo. Always scale it in proportion (hold Shift while resizing).

> **Don't** rotate, tilt or flip the logo, or set it on a curve.

> **Don't** recolour any part of it, swap Harbour Blue and Sunset Coral, or use tints, gradients or colours from outside our palette.

> **Don't** add shadows, glows, bevels, outlines, textures or 3D effects.

> **Don't** move, resize or separate the symbol and wordmark, or rearrange them into a new stacked or centred lock-up.

> **Don't** use the plain single-colour version that fills the whole circle; it erases the sun and waves. Use the knockout version.

> **Don't** put the full-colour logo on any background other than white, or any version on a busy photo or a background with less than 3:1 contrast.

> **Don't** put the logo inside a shape, badge, frame or speech bubble that is not shown in this guide.

> **Don't** retype "Harbourside Community Trust" in another font next to the symbol, or redraw the wordmark.

> **Don't** crop the logo, let a frame or page edge cut through it, or use it below its minimum size.

> **Don't** use old, screenshotted, blurry or low-resolution copies. Download the current files from the asset library.

### Logo accessibility
- **Alt text:** use the alt text in the specifications table above, "Harbourside Community Trust logo". When the logo links to our home page, use "Harbourside Community Trust home" so screen reader users know where the link goes.

#### Nunito: display

Nunito is a rounded sans-serif. Its soft, rounded stroke ends echo the heavy, rounded letters of "Harbourside" in our logo, so headlines feel part of the same family. (We have not identified the typeface used in the logo itself; Nunito is chosen because it is similar in feel, not because it is the same font.)

- Use **ExtraBold (800)** or **Bold (700)** for the `display`, `h1` and `h2` styles, and **SemiBold (600)** for `h3`.
- Use **Regular (400)** only for large pull quotes.
- Don't use the Light and ExtraLight weights: thin strokes are hard to read for people with low vision.

#### Atkinson Hyperlegible Next: body

Atkinson Hyperlegible Next was designed with the Braille Institute for readers with low vision. Letters that are often confused, such as capital I, lower-case l and the number 1, or capital O and zero, are drawn to look clearly different. That makes it a strong fit for older residents and anyone reading in a second language.

- Use **Regular (400)** for body text, **SemiBold (600)** for labels and `h4`, and **Bold (700)** for emphasis.
- Use its true italic for titles and terms only.

#### JetBrains Mono: code and reference numbers

Use JetBrains Mono, with the `code` style, only for reference numbers, case IDs, code and data tables on our website. Don't use it for headings or body text.

| Email newsletters and signatures | Arial, Bold | Verdana, Arial, sans-serif |

We choose Verdana for body text because, like Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, it is wide and open, with letters that are easy to tell apart at small sizes. Verdana runs wider than our body font, so expect longer documents; reduce the size by no more than one point (and never below the minimums in "Readable text").

> **Don't** send Word or PowerPoint files that rely on our fonts to partners who may not have them. Export a PDF with fonts embedded, or use the substitute fonts.

### Type scale and hierarchy

- display — 60px / 3.75rem, line height 1.1, weight 700, tracking -0.02em. Campaign headlines, hero banners (one per page)
- h1 — 48px / 3rem, line height 1.15, weight 700, tracking -0.015em. Page titles
- h2 — 36px / 2.25rem, line height 1.2, weight 700, tracking -0.01em. Section titles
- h3 — 28px / 1.75rem, line height 1.25, weight 600, tracking 0. Sub-sections
- h4 — 22px / 1.375rem, line height 1.3, weight 600, tracking 0. Card and component titles
- body-lg — 20px / 1.25rem, line height 1.5, weight 400, tracking 0. Introductions and lead paragraphs

- small — 14px / 0.875rem, line height 1.5, weight 400, tracking 0.01em. Captions, metadata, legal (not for long passages)
- label — 14px / 0.875rem, line height 1.4, weight 600, tracking 0.02em. Buttons, form labels, tags

The scale goes up in steps of about 1.25, from 16px body text. Each style has one job:

- **`display`:** one campaign headline or hero banner per page.
- **`h1`:** the page or document title. Exactly one per page.
- **`h2`, `h3`, `h4`:** sections, sub-sections and card titles. Don't skip levels: an `h3` always sits inside an `h2`.
- **`body-lg`:** the first paragraph of an article, or short introductions.
- **`body`:** all running text.
- **`small`:** captions, dates and legal notes. Never for whole paragraphs.
- **`label`:** buttons, form labels and tags.

Choose heading levels for structure, not for size. Headings should describe what follows ("How to apply for a food parcel", not "Next steps"), because many people jump between headings with a screen reader or by scanning.

On screens narrower than 768px, *proposed:* reduce `display` to 40px, `h1` to 36px, `h2` to 28px and `h3` to 24px, keeping weights and line heights. Body sizes stay the same.

### Readable text

- **Line length:** keep lines to the measure in the type settings, 45–75 characters (about 66 is ideal). On wide screens, limit the text column, not the font size.
- **Line height:** at least 1.5 for body text; 1.1–1.3 for headings, as set in the scale.
- **Paragraph spacing:** leave at least one line of space between paragraphs (`space-4` or more on screen).
- **Alignment:** left-aligned, with a ragged right edge. Never justify text: it creates uneven gaps that make reading harder, especially for people with dyslexia.
- **Capitals:** use sentence case for headings and buttons. Avoid all caps except for a short label of no more than three words.
- **Italics:** no more than one sentence at a time.

#### Minimum sizes

| Medium | Body text | Smallest text |
|---|---|---|
| Websites and email | 16px (`body`) | 14px (`small`) |
| Printed letters, leaflets and posters for families and residents | 14pt | 10pt for footnotes and legal lines |
| Reports for partners and internal documents | 12pt | 9pt for footnotes |
| Large-print versions (on request) | 16pt or larger | 14pt |
| Posters and signs | See chapter 18 | — |
| Text on social images | At least 4% of the image height | — |
| Slides | 24pt | 18pt |

Because many readers are older, never set public materials below 14pt body text, and never use 11pt for body text anywhere.

### Emphasis

- Use **bold** for emphasis, sparingly.
- Use *italics* only for titles of publications and to introduce a term.
- Underline is reserved for links. Never underline text for emphasis.
- Don't use colour alone for emphasis: a word in Harbour Blue is not emphasised for someone who can't see the difference.

### Numbers

- In tables, financial summaries and charts, use tabular (equal-width) figures so columns line up. Check that the font supports them in your software (in CSS, `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums`); if not, right-align the numbers.
- Use lining figures (the default) in interface text.
- Follow the date, time, money and phone number formats in chapter 12.

### Languages

We work with families who speak many languages. Before publishing in any language other than English, check that every character displays correctly.

- **Latin-script languages** such as Polish, Romanian, Lithuanian, Portuguese, Turkish and Vietnamese: both Nunito and Atkinson Hyperlegible Next are published with extended Latin character sets, but check the specific letters before you print, for example Polish ą ę ł ś ź ż, Romanian ș ț (with a comma below, not a cedilla) and Turkish ğ ı ş. Look at the "Glyphs" or "Language support" section on the Google Fonts specimen page.
- **Cyrillic** (for example Ukrainian or Russian): check coverage for each font on its specimen page. If a font lacks Cyrillic, use Noto Sans for that language.
- **Scripts our fonts don't cover**, such as Arabic, Urdu, Persian, Kurdish (Sorani), Bengali, Punjabi (Gurmukhi), Tamil, Amharic or Tigrinya, and Chinese: use the matching **Noto Sans** family (for example Noto Sans Arabic, Noto Sans Bengali, Noto Sans SC), which is also free under the SIL Open Font License. Keep our English headings in Nunito if the page is bilingual.
- **Right-to-left languages** (Arabic, Urdu, Persian): align text to the right, mirror the layout (navigation, icons that point forwards), never add letter-spacing (it breaks joined letters), and don't use italics. Ask a native speaker to check the layout before printing.
- **Translations are often longer than English** (often 20–30% longer). Leave room in layouts rather than shrinking the text.
- **Hyphenation:** turn automatic hyphenation off in English. For other languages, ask the translator.

### Typography accessibility
- Text must stay readable when zoomed to 200%, and websites must allow people to change line, letter and word spacing without content being cut off.
- Never use images of text. Posters and graphics shared online must repeat their words in the post text or alt text.
- For readers with dyslexia, spacing, left alignment, short lines and plain words matter more than any "special" font. Our body font and these rules already serve them well.
- Keep contrast to the approved pairings in chapter 04.

### Typography: do and don't
> **Do** use Nunito for headlines and Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for everything people need to read.

> **Do** set body text at 16px on screen, 14pt in print for families and residents, and 12pt for partner reports.

> **Don't** use more than two typefaces in one design (JetBrains Mono for reference numbers is the only exception).

> **Don't** set long passages in Nunito below 20px, track out (letter-space) body text, or squash text to fit.

> **Don't** use your software's fake bold or fake italic buttons with a font that lacks those styles; install the real weights.

### Composition

- **One focal point per layout:** a headline, a photo or a key number, not all three competing.
- **White space is part of the design:** it gives the page the calm, uncluttered feel of our brand. Don't fill every gap.
- **Clear hierarchy:** headline, then supporting text, then the action. Put the most important information, such as a phone number or opening times, where people look first.
- **Consistent alignment:** left-align text blocks to the grid. Centre only short headlines on posters, social cards and end slides.

### Supporting graphic: the wave line

*Proposed — confirm with the Communications team:* the three wave lines from the symbol may be used on their own as a section divider or the edge of a panel, in Tide Blue, Harbour Blue or white. A designer should draw this from vector artwork. Never use the sun on its own as a graphic, or the wave line where it could be mistaken for the logo; and never place text on top of it.

### Layout accessibility
- **Reflow:** web pages must work at 320px wide without horizontal scrolling, except for data tables, maps and diagrams.
- **Zoom:** text must remain readable when zoomed to 400%, with no horizontal scrolling for text blocks.
- **Order:** the reading order and keyboard focus order follow the visual order. Don't use layout tricks that place content visually in a different order from the code or the document structure.
- **Targets:** keep at least `space-2` between tappable items, and make buttons and controls at least 44 by 44px on all screens.
- **Print:** keep text out of the bleed and fold areas.

> **Do** use generous white space and the `space-*` tokens to group related content.

> **Don't** squeeze extra content in by shrinking margins, line spacing or text size.


## Iconography

Icons help people find what they need quickly, including people who read English as a second language or find long text tiring. They only help if they are simple, consistent and always paired with words.

### Our icon style

Our icons match the rounded, friendly character of the logo:

- **Outline style** with a **2px stroke** at 24px.
- **Rounded line ends and rounded corners**, like the wave lines in our symbol.
- Drawn on a **24px grid with 2px padding**, so the artwork sits inside a 20px live area.
- **Filled** versions only for a selected state (for example, the active tab in a menu).
- **Simple and literal:** one idea per icon, no tiny details, no text inside the icon.

### Recommended icon set

Use **[Lucide icons](https://lucide.dev)**. Its rounded line ends, rounded joins and 2px stroke on a 24px grid match our style without changes. Lucide is free and open source under the ISC licence, which allows commercial use; keep the licence notice with any code that includes it.

If an icon is missing from Lucide, draw a new one in the same style (24px grid, 2px stroke, rounded ends and joins) and add it to the asset library. Don't mix icons from different sets in one design.

### One icon per meaning

Use the same icon for the same idea everywhere, so people learn it once.

| Meaning | Lucide icon |
|---|---|
| Housing and homes | `house` |
| Food and meals | `utensils` |
| Work and training | `briefcase` |
| Phone us | `phone` |
| Email us | `mail` |
| Find us / location | `map-pin` |
| Opening times | `clock` |
| Volunteering | `hand-heart` |
| Donate | `heart` |
| Information | `info` |
| Warning | `triangle-alert` |
| Error | `circle-x` |
| Success | `circle-check` |

Avoid hand gestures (thumbs up, OK sign) and culturally specific symbols: their meaning varies across the communities we serve.

### Sizes

| Size | Use |
|---|---|
| 16px | Inline with `small` text only, always next to a word |
| 20px | Inline with `body` text, buttons and form fields |
| 24px | Default: navigation, cards, lists |
| 32px | Service tiles and feature lists |
| 48px and up | Posters, leaflets and signage pictograms |

Scale icons as whole units; don't change the stroke width. At 32px and above, the stroke scales with the icon. In print, keep icons at least 6mm high.

### Colour

- Use `text-default` (`neutral-900`) or Harbour Blue on white and light backgrounds.
- Use white on Harbour Blue and on `neutral-950` backgrounds.
- Sunset Coral icons on white must use `secondary-500` or darker. Exact Sunset Coral and exact Tide Blue are below 3:1 on white, so use them only for decorative icons that add no information.
- Status icons use their state colour from chapter 04, always with a text label.
- Meaningful icons need at least 3:1 against their background.

### Icon accessibility
- **Labels:** show a visible text label with every icon wherever there is room. Many of our readers will understand "Food" faster than a fork and knife.
- **Icon-only buttons** (for example, close or search) need an accessible name, such as `aria-label="Close"`, and a tooltip on hover and focus.
- **Decorative icons** next to a text label are hidden from screen readers (`aria-hidden="true"`), so the label isn't read twice.
- **Never use an icon alone for critical meaning**, such as an error, a deadline or a cost.
- **Target size:** the clickable area is at least 44 by 44px on all screens, even if the icon itself is smaller. This is stricter than the WCAG minimum of 24 by 24px because many of our users are older.

> **Do** pair every icon with a short, plain word: "Call us", "Food", "Opening times".

> **Do** use the same icon for the same meaning on the website, leaflets and social media.

> **Don't** mix outline and filled icons, or icons from different sets, in one design.

> **Don't** use exact Sunset Coral or Tide Blue for icons that carry meaning on white.


## Photography and illustration

The people in our pictures are our neighbours. Every image we publish should show them with dignity, as capable people in their own community, never as objects of pity. That is how we earn the trust of families, volunteers, donors and partners alike.

### Our photography style

- **Real people, real places:** families, volunteers and staff in Harbourside's streets, community spaces, kitchens and seafront, photographed with their consent.
- **Candid and warm:** people doing things together (cooking, talking, learning, moving into a new home), rather than posing for the camera.
- **Coastal light:** natural daylight, open skies and soft morning or evening light that echoes the rising sun in our logo.
- **Close and human:** eye-level framing, hands at work, shared moments. Leave calm space (sky, sea, a plain wall) where a headline or the logo can go.

#### Colour and treatment

- Grade gently towards warm, natural tones and clear blues. Never change skin tones to match brand colours.
- No heavy filters, duotones over faces, vignettes or artificial lens flare.
- Crop to the story, but never crop through people's joints or faces in a way that removes their identity or dignity.

### Representation

Our community is diverse and our pictures should show it, without tokenism.

- Show a range of ages, with **older residents** as active, independent people, not only as recipients of help.
- Show different ethnicities, faiths, body types, genders and family shapes, as they really are in Harbourside.
- Show disabled people as active participants: volunteering, working, leading.
- Show volunteers and the people we support side by side, as equals. Avoid "helper and helped" compositions where one person looks down on another.
- Review a campaign's images together: does the set, as a whole, reflect the community?

### Dignity and consent

These rules protect the people we work with. They are not optional.

- **Written consent** for every identifiable person, explained in plain language and, where needed, in their first language or with an interpreter. Consent forms must say where the image will be used and for how long.
- **Children** under 18 need consent from a parent or guardian. Don't publish a child's full name with their photo.
- **People can withdraw consent.** Remove their images from our channels promptly when asked, and keep a record.
- **Don't show identifying details** of anyone at risk: house numbers, street signs, car registrations, school uniforms or documents. Never identify anyone fleeing abuse or homelessness; use silhouettes, hands or backs instead.
- **No poverty images:** don't show empty cupboards, crying children, or people in distress to raise money. Show the change our support makes instead.
- *Proposed — confirm with the Communications team:* review consent for every image in the library every two years, and remove images whose consent has expired.

### What to avoid

- Staged stock photos of people pointing at laptops or shaking hands.
- Images that don't look like Harbourside (for example, tropical beaches or city skyscrapers).
- Text baked into photos. Put text in the layout or the caption so it can be translated, read by screen readers and resized.
- Photos with logos or brands of other organisations in clear view, unless agreed with them.

### Text over images

- Place text over a photo only on a solid panel (Harbour Blue with white text, or white with `neutral-900` text) or over a dark scrim.
- If you use a scrim, check contrast at the lightest point behind the text: it must reach 4.5:1 for body text, or 3:1 for large text.
- Never place text over faces or busy detail.
- On social media, repeat any text in the image in the post copy and the alt text.

### Illustration

Use illustration when photographs aren't suitable, for example for sensitive subjects or to explain a process.

- **Style:** simple, flat shapes with rounded corners and rounded line ends, matching our icons; a 2px line weight at 24px scale, scaled up in proportion.
- **Colour:** fills from the brand scales (Harbour Blue, Sunset Coral and Tide Blue tints) on white or light neutral backgrounds. Any lines or shapes that carry meaning must reach 3:1 against their background.
- **People:** friendly and simplified, with a range of skin tones, ages and abilities. Don't draw caricatures.
- **Commissioning:** brief illustrators with this chapter and the colour chapter, and own or license the artwork for all our channels.

### Sourcing and rights

- **Our own photography first.** Brief photographers with this chapter and get model releases signed on the day.
- **Stock images** only from licensed libraries, and only when they look authentic and local. Check that the licence covers web, print and social media, and keep a copy of it.
- **Credit** photographers when the licence or agreement asks for it, in the format "Photo: Name".
- Store every image in the asset library with its consent form, licence, photographer and expiry date.

### Alt text

- Describe the **purpose** of the image, not every detail: "Volunteers serving lunch at the Harbourside community kitchen."
- Keep it to about 125 characters. Put longer explanations in the caption or body text.
- Don't start with "Image of" or "Photo of".
- Name people only if they have consented to be named.
- Purely decorative images get empty alt text (`alt=""`).

### AI-generated imagery

- **Never** use AI to create realistic images of the families, volunteers or staff we work with, or of anyone who looks like a real beneficiary. Our stories must be true.
- AI images may be used only for clearly illustrative or abstract graphics (for example, a background texture), approved by the Communications team.
- **Disclose** AI-generated images in the caption or credit ("Image created with AI"), and follow each social platform's labelling rules.
- Check AI images for bias, stereotypes and visual errors (hands, text, faces) before use.
- Don't use AI tools to edit real photographs in ways that change what happened, such as adding people or removing signs of hardship.

> **Do** show people doing things together, in natural light, in places that look like Harbourside.

> **Don't** use images of distress or poverty to make people feel guilty, or publish anyone's photo without their written consent.

> **Accessibility** Every meaningful image needs alt text that explains why it is there, and no image should contain text that isn't also available as real text.


## Data visualisation

Charts in our annual report, funding bids and reports to council partners show the difference our work makes. They must be accurate, easy to read at a glance and readable by everyone, including people who can't see colour differences and people using screen readers.

### Choosing a chart

- **Bar charts** for comparing amounts (meals served per neighbourhood). Bars run horizontally when labels are long.
- **Line charts** for change over time (families housed each month).
- **A single big number** with a short sentence when there is only one figure to show.
- **Tables** when people need exact values.
- Avoid pie and doughnut charts with more than five slices, 3D charts, and dual axes.
- Always start bar chart axes at zero. Show the source and date of the data under every chart.

### Series colours

Use colours in this order, so every chart in a report looks consistent:

| Order | On white | On dark (`neutral-950`) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Harbour Blue (`primary-700`) | `primary-500` |
| 2 | `secondary-500` | Sunset Coral (`secondary-400`) |
| 3 | `accent-600` | `accent-200` |
| 4 | `neutral-800` | `neutral-400` |

Every colour in this order reaches at least 3:1 against its background. We checked the set with colour-vision simulations and no pair was flagged as confusable. In greyscale, though, Harbour Blue and `neutral-800` look similar, so add patterns or labels when charts may be printed in black and white.

- **Up to four series.** If you need more, split the chart into small multiples or use a table.
- **Highlight, then mute:** to make one point, show the key series in Harbour Blue and the others in `neutral-500`.
- **Neighbouring colours don't reach 3:1 with each other**, so separate adjacent bars, stacked segments and slices with a 2px white gap (or a `neutral-950` gap in dark mode).
- Don't use exact Tide Blue or exact Sunset Coral for marks on white; they are below 3:1. They may be used for background bands or highlight areas behind data.
- Never use the success, warning or error colours as series colours, and never use red and green as the only difference.

### Labels, patterns and legends

- **Label directly:** put series names at the end of lines or next to bars instead of in a separate colour legend.
- **Patterns for print:** in printed reports, add a pattern (diagonal lines, dots) or a different marker shape (circle, square, triangle) to each series after the first.
- **Show the numbers:** label key values on bars or points so readers don't need to estimate.
- **Gridlines:** few and light, in `border-subtle`. They guide the eye but carry no meaning.

### Typography in charts

- Use Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for all chart text.
- Labels at least 14px on screen (`small`) and 10pt in print (9pt only in partner reports). Titles use `h4` on screen.
- Use tabular figures, and format numbers, dates and currency as set out in chapter 12.
- Write chart titles as the key finding: "We served 20% more meals in winter" rather than "Meals by month". (This example is illustrative, not real data.)
- Keep all text horizontal; don't rotate axis labels.

### Text alternatives

Every chart needs a text alternative, because a chart image is invisible to screen readers and hard to read for many people.

- **Alt text or caption** with three parts: the type of chart, what it shows, and the key takeaway. For example: "Bar chart of meals served in each neighbourhood in 2025. The harbour front served the most."
- **A data table** next to the chart or in an appendix, with a header row and units in the column headings.
- For council partners, provide the underlying data as a spreadsheet (CSV or XLSX) on request.
- In PDFs, tag charts as figures with alt text (chapter 17).

> **Do** label data directly, show the source, and give every chart a plain-language takeaway.

> **Don't** rely on a colour key alone, use 3D effects, or cut a bar chart's axis above zero.

> **Accessibility** Chart marks need at least 3:1 against the background, and meaning must also be shown with labels, patterns or position, never colour alone.


## Motion

Movement should feel like our logo: a calm tide and a slowly rising sun, never a jolt. We use motion only to help people understand what is happening, and we always respect people who need less of it.

### Principles

- **Purposeful:** motion explains a change, such as a menu opening, a message appearing or a step completing. Never animate just to decorate.
- **Gentle and quick:** short movements with a soft ease-out, so things settle into place like water coming to rest. No bouncing, except a single small overshoot for a celebration (for example, "Thank you for your donation").
- **Consistent direction:** things enter from where they will be and leave the way they came. Panels slide from the side they are attached to; messages rise up gently from the bottom.
- **Small distances:** move items by no more than `space-5` to `space-6`. Large sweeps across the screen are tiring and can cause motion sickness.

### Durations and easing

**Motion tokens**

| Token | Value |
|---|---|

## Voice and tone

The people who read our words may be worried about rent, food or work. Some are older, and many speak English as a second language. Our voice must make them feel welcome, respected and sure of what to do next.

Our **voice** is who we are, and it stays the same everywhere. Our **tone** changes with the situation, the way a kind neighbour speaks differently at a party and at a hospital bedside.

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* the voice attributes, tone settings and word list below. They are built from our mission, our four values and the personality in chapter 2.

### Voice attributes

We have four voice attributes. Each one links to a personality trait in chapter 2.

#### 1. Warm, not sentimental

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Friendly, kind and welcoming. We talk to people as equals. | Gushing, pitying or dramatic. We never make people feel like a "case". |

**Example:** "Come along on Tuesday. We will make you a cup of tea, and someone will help you with the form."

#### 2. Clear and simple, not childish

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Plain, direct and easy to translate. We use short sentences and everyday words. | Babyish, vague or full of jargon. Simple words do not mean simple ideas. |

**Example:** "You can get a food parcel if you live in Harbourside. Bring something with your address on it, such as a letter."

#### 3. Calm and steady, not cold

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Reassuring and reliable. We say what will happen next. | Formal, distant or alarmed. We do not use official-sounding phrases or shout in capitals. |

**Example:** "If you are worried about losing your home, call us. We will listen, and we will help you work out your next step."

#### 4. Hopeful and honest, not over-promising

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Positive about what people can do together. Open about what we can and cannot do. | Promising outcomes we cannot guarantee. Exaggerating need or results. |

**Example:** "We cannot find everyone a home straight away. But we will stay with you while you look."

> **Do** read your words aloud. If they sound like a friendly person from Harbourside, the voice is right.

> **Don't** borrow the voice of a bank, a council letter or a fundraising advert. We are none of those.

#### Crisis and bad news checklist

When something goes wrong, people need facts fast. Follow these steps:

1. Say what has happened in the first sentence.
2. Say what it means for the reader.
3. Say what they should do now, and where to get help.
4. Say when we will update them.
5. Get approval from the Communications team before you post.

> **Don't** use humour, emoji or campaign hashtags during a crisis or when someone has made a complaint.

### Writing about people and money

> **Don't** show people as helpless: "Poor Amira had nowhere to turn until we rescued her."

## Writing style

Consistent, plain writing helps people trust us and act on what we say. Many of our readers are older residents or speak English as a second language. If a sentence is easy for them, it is easy for everyone.

These rules apply to everything we publish: leaflets, posters, the website, social posts, WhatsApp messages, emails, letters and reports.

### Plain English comes first

Plain English is our most important writing rule. When another rule in this chapter conflicts with it, plain English wins.

#### Our targets

| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| Reading age for public content | 9 to 11 years (UK) |
| Average sentence length | 15 words or fewer |
| Longest sentence | 25 words |
| Ideas per sentence | One |
| Paragraph length | 3 sentences or fewer on screen |

Council partner reports and funding bids can go a little higher, but aim for a reading age of 12 to 14 and still define every technical term.

#### How to write plainly

- **Put the most important thing first.** Start with what the reader needs to know or do.
- **Use the active voice.** "We will call you on Monday", not "You will be contacted on Monday".
- **Talk to the reader as "you".** Speak as "we" for Harbourside Community Trust.
- **Use everyday words.** "Help", not "assist". "Use", not "utilise". "About", not "approximately". "Get", not "obtain". "Start", not "commence".
- **Use the same word for the same thing.** Do not switch between "food parcel", "food box" and "food bag".
- **Explain what will happen next,** and how long it will take.
- **Test your writing.** Read it aloud. Use a readability checker for long pages. Best of all, ask a local resident to read it.

#### Avoid idioms and figures of speech

Idioms are phrases that do not mean what the words say. They confuse readers with English as a second language and do not translate well.

| Avoid | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "We've got your back" | "We will support you" |
| "Get stuck in" | "Join in" or "Help us" |
| "Make ends meet" | "Pay for the things you need" |
| "On the breadline" | "On a low income" |
| "Keep a roof over your head" | "Stay in your home" |
| "Drop us a line" | "Email us" or "Call us" |
| "Hit the ground running" | "Start straight away" |

#### Define terms

If you must use a technical word, explain it the first time, in plain words. For example: "Universal Credit is a payment from the government to help with living costs."

> **Do** write: "Bring a letter with your address on it, such as a gas or electricity bill."

> **Don't** write: "Proof of residency documentation must be provided at point of access."

### Translations and Easy Read

Many families in Harbourside read English as a second language. Plan for this from the start.

- **Write the English version plainly first.** Plain English is quicker, cheaper and more accurate to translate.
- **Use professional or community-checked translations** for important information about homes, food, money or safety. Do not rely only on automatic translation for these.
- **Avoid text in images.** Translation tools and screen readers cannot read it. Put the words in the post or on the page.
- **Leave space in layouts.** Some languages need up to 30% more space than English.
- **Offer Easy Read versions** of key information, such as how to get help. Easy Read uses very short sentences, one idea per line and a clear picture beside each idea.
- **Show which languages are available** in each language's own name and script, not only in English.

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* which community languages we translate into. We do not yet have this list. Ask families and partners which languages are most needed.

### Inclusive and dignity-first language

We describe people with respect. Every family we work with is a neighbour, not a problem to solve.

#### Poverty, housing and food

- Describe the situation, not the person: "families on a low income", "people who are homeless", "people who need help with food".
- Do not use "the needy", "the poor", "the less fortunate", "handouts", "benefit scroungers" or "deprived families".
- Do not use words of pity: "poor", "desperate", "helpless", "suffering", "pitiful", "victims".
- Show people as active and capable: "Sam is looking for work", not "Sam is unemployed and struggling".
- Do not describe a person's home, clothes or appearance unless it is essential and they agree.

#### Disability, age, gender and culture

- Follow how people describe themselves. In the UK, "disabled people" (identity-first) is widely preferred. Some people prefer "people with a disability". Ask, and follow their choice.
- Do not use "suffers from", "wheelchair-bound", "the disabled" or "normal" (meaning not disabled).
- Avoid disability metaphors: "blind spot", "falling on deaf ears", "crippling costs", "lame", "crazy". Write "gap", "ignored", "very high costs" instead.
- Use gender-neutral words: "they", "everyone", "parents and carers", "chair", "staff".
- Say "older people" or "older residents", not "the elderly", "pensioners" (as a label) or "OAPs".
- Do not mention someone's age, race, religion, nationality, immigration status or health unless it is relevant and they agree.
- Avoid sports, military and festival-specific idioms that not everyone shares.

> **Do** write: "Parents and carers can bring children to the warm meal."

> **Don't** write: "Mums can bring the kids along."

### Stories, consent and personal data

Stories help people understand our work. They also involve personal data, which the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) protects.

- **Get written consent** before you use anyone's name, photo, voice, story or quotes. For children under 18, get consent from a parent or carer, and ask the child too.
- **Explain clearly** where the story will appear (for example, Facebook, the website, a leaflet), for how long, and that it may be shared further online.
- **Make it easy to say no** and to change their mind later. Tell them how: "Email comms@harbourside.example and we will remove it."
- **Change names and details** when someone wants to stay anonymous. Say so: "Name changed to protect privacy."
- **Never share** addresses, health details, immigration status or details that could identify someone without clear, specific consent.
- **Do not ask for personal details in public** social comments. Move the conversation to a private message.
- **Keep consent records** with the Communications team.

*Proposed — confirm with leadership:* the consent form, how long stories stay published and how long consent records are kept. These need checking against the organisation's data protection policy.

### Asking for donations

We ask people to give because they can make a positive difference, never because they should feel guilty.

- Be honest about what money pays for. Use only real, checked figures.
- Say "Give if you can". Never say "Only you can save" or "before it's too late" unless a real deadline exists.
- Do not use shocking or pitying descriptions of families.
- Make it just as easy to stop a regular gift as to start one.
- Ask before adding people to email or WhatsApp lists, and say how often we will contact them.

### Our name

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Full name, first mention | Harbourside Community Trust |
| Short name, after first mention | the Trust |
| Possessive | Harbourside Community Trust's volunteers |
| Verb agreement | Singular: "Harbourside Community Trust is a local charity." |

- Write the full name the first time on every page, post or document.
- After that, you may write "the Trust" (lower-case "the", capital "T"), or "we".
- Do not shorten the name to "HCT" in public content. If a partner document needs an abbreviation, write the full name first with the abbreviation in brackets.
- Do not split or restyle the name: never "Harbour Side", "HarbourSide" or all capitals.
- Write "Harbourside" (one word, capital H) for the place too.

### Spelling, grammar and punctuation

| Topic | Our rule |
|---|---|
| Spelling | British English (en-GB): colour, organisation, centre, neighbour, programme (an event) |
| Dictionary | *Proposed — confirm with leadership:* Oxford English Dictionary or Collins |

- Use **sentence case** for headings, buttons, menus, labels, email subjects and posters: "Get help with food", not "Get Help With Food".

- Use only one main call to action in each message.
- Give a way to act without the internet, such as a phone number or a place to visit.

- Introduce the list with a short sentence.
- Start each item with a capital letter. Use a full stop only if the item is a full sentence.
- Keep lists to seven items or fewer.

- **Use meaningful headings** in order, so people can scan the page and screen-reader users can jump between sections.
- **Do not rely on colour, shape or position.** Write "Select 'Book a place'", not "Click the coral button on the right".

- **Emoji:** use one or two at most, at the end of a sentence. Never use emoji to replace words, and never use them in serious or bad news.
- **Do not use "fancy" Unicode letters** (𝐛𝐨𝐥𝐝 or 𝒾𝓉𝒶𝓁𝒾𝒸 text). Screen readers cannot read them.

> **Do** write: "Read our guide to help with energy bills (PDF, 400 KB)."

> **Don't** write: "Click here for help 👉👉 #energybills #cost #help #harbourside #winter"

2. Are sentences 25 words or fewer, with no idioms?
3. Have you described people with dignity and checked consent?
4. Is every fact, number and date checked?
5. Do links and buttons say what they do?
6. Is there a way to get help without the internet?
7. Has someone else read it?


## Accessibility

Many of the people we serve are older residents. Many others speak English as a second language, have a disability, or rarely go online. If they cannot read, hear or use something we make, we have not helped them. Accessibility is part of being kind, fair and local first, so this chapter applies to everyone who makes anything for Harbourside Community Trust.

### Our commitment

Everything we publish meets **WCAG 2.2 level AA**. That includes our website, Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn and WhatsApp posts, documents and PDFs, letters, posters, signs, videos and events. Where this chapter sets a higher bar than AA, such as larger touch targets or larger print, we follow the higher bar because of who our audiences are.

We always offer a way to get help that does not need the internet. Every leaflet, post, page and letter that asks people to do something also gives a phone number or an in-person option.

#### The law in the UK

- **Equality Act 2010:** as a service provider, we must make reasonable adjustments for disabled people. We should plan for them in advance, not only when someone asks. This applies to us now.
- **Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018:** these apply to our council partners. Charities are usually exempt. They can apply to a charity that is mostly publicly funded, or that provides services which are essential to the public or aimed mainly at disabled people. *Proposed — confirm with the trustees whether they apply to us.* Either way, council contracts may require us to meet WCAG 2.2 AA and publish an accessibility statement, and we do both.

> **Note** We publish an accessibility statement on our website. It says how well the site meets WCAG 2.2 AA, lists known problems, and explains how to report a problem or ask for another format. The Communications team reviews it every 12 months.

### Colour and contrast

Contrast is the difference in brightness between text and its background. Many older readers find low contrast hard to read, especially in daylight or on a cracked phone screen.

| What | Minimum contrast | WCAG 2.2 |
|---|---|---|
| Text under 24px (or under 18.66px bold) | 4.5:1 | 1.4.3 |
| Large text, 24px or more (or 18.66px bold or more) | 3:1 | 1.4.3 |
| Buttons, form borders, icons that carry meaning, chart lines and bars | 3:1 against what is next to them | 1.4.11 |
| Focus indicators | 3:1, at least 2px thick | 1.4.11, 2.4.7 |

The approved colour pairings are listed in the Colour chapter (chapter 4). Use those first. Our brand colours behave differently:

- **Harbour Blue** passes for text of any size on white.
- **Sunset Coral** is 2.95:1 on white. Use it only for decoration and large shapes. For coral text, links or icons on white, use `secondary-600`.
- **Tide Blue** is 1.77:1 on white. Use it only for decoration and backgrounds behind dark text. For text, use `accent-600`.

The full-colour logo on white is an approved exception, because logos are exempt from the contrast rule. "Community Trust" in Sunset Coral is hard for some people to read, so the logo must never be the only place our name appears. Always write "Harbourside Community Trust" in real text nearby, for example in the page title, the first line of a post or the letter heading.

#### Checking a new combination

1. Look for the pairing in chapter 4.
2. If it is not there, check it with any WCAG contrast checker, such as the WebAIM Contrast Checker.
3. The brand team can also run `contrast_check.py FG BG --use body` (or `--use large`, or `--use ui`). The script fails any pairing below the threshold.
4. Ask the Communications team to add new approved pairings to the brand data. Do not use an unapproved pairing in the meantime.

For text over photos, put the text on a solid panel or a dark scrim. Measure the contrast against the busiest, lightest part of the photo, not the average.

> **Do** use `secondary-600` for coral text and Harbour Blue for links on white backgrounds.

> **Don't** set text in Sunset Coral or Tide Blue on white, even in headlines.

### Do not rely on colour alone

About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women see colour differently. Others print in black and white or use high-contrast settings. Colour must never be the only way to show something (WCAG 1.4.1).

- **Links in body text** are always underlined.
- **Errors, warnings and success messages** have an icon and a text label, such as "Error: enter your postcode". Keep Sunset Coral out of error messages: it looks like the error colour. Keep Harbour Blue and Tide Blue out of information messages for the same reason.
- **States** such as selected, required or unavailable are shown in words or with an icon as well as with colour.
- **Charts and maps** label each series or area directly, or use patterns and different line styles. Success, warning and error colours look alike to many people with colour-vision deficiency, and so do Sunset Coral and the success green. Never let a reader tell these apart by colour alone. See Data visualisation (chapter 9).

> **Don't** write "the items in red are full". Write "Full" next to each item.

### Typography

Our body typeface, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, was designed for people with low vision. Its letters, such as I, l and 1, are easy to tell apart. Set it well, or the benefit is lost.

| Medium | Body text | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Web and apps | `body` (16px) minimum. Use `body-lg` (20px) for pages aimed at older residents | Nothing below 14px (`small`), and only for captions and legal lines |
| Social images | Text at least 4% of the image height (54px on a 1080 × 1350 post) | Repeat the words in the caption |
| Letters and leaflets for the public | 14pt minimum | 12pt only for partner and internal reports |
| Large print | 16pt minimum | Supply on request, and offer it by default at events for older people |
| Posters and signs | Readable from the expected viewing distance | See Environment and merchandise (chapter 18) |

- Line height at least 1.5 for body text, with paragraph spacing at least 1.5 times the text size.
- Lines of 45–75 characters.
- Text is left-aligned and **never justified**. Justified text creates uneven gaps that make reading harder for people with dyslexia.
- Write headings in sentence case. Do not set more than a few words in capitals, and do not put whole paragraphs in italics or light weights.
- Pages must work when people zoom to 200% and when the screen is 320px wide, with nothing cut off and no sideways scrolling (WCAG 1.4.4, 1.4.10). Layouts must cope when people increase letter, word and line spacing (1.4.12).
- Do not use pictures of text, except the logo (1.4.5).

#### Plain English, Easy Read and translations

Write for a reading age of 9 to 11. Use short sentences, common words and the active voice. Avoid idioms such as "touch base" or "in the same boat", which do not translate and confuse readers new to English. Explain any acronym the first time you use it.

- Produce **Easy Read** versions of important information, such as how to get help with housing, food or work. Easy Read uses short sentences, each paired with a supporting picture.
- Offer **translations** of key information. *Proposed — confirm the community languages with frontline staff.* Use professional or community-checked translations, not unchecked machine translation. On the web, mark the language of a translated page or passage in the code (WCAG 3.1.1, 3.1.2).
- Always put a text label with an icon. An icon alone can mean different things to different people.

The focus indicator shows keyboard users where they are on a page. Never remove it. Our focus style, including the colours to use on brand-colour backgrounds and in dark mode:

Focus ring: 3px solid, 2px offset; `#356db2` on light, `#4c88d3` on dark surfaces.

- Everything that works with a mouse must also work with a keyboard alone. Keyboard focus must never get stuck (2.1.1, 2.1.2).
- Focus moves in a logical order and is never hidden behind sticky headers or cookie banners (2.4.3, 2.4.11).
- **Targets:** WCAG requires at least 24 by 24px (2.5.8). Because many of our users have tremor or limited dexterity, **we make every button and standalone control at least 44 by 44px**, with space between them. Inline text links cannot be that size, so give them generous line spacing (at least 1.5) and keep them apart from other links.
- Do not make people drag, swipe or use two fingers. Offer a simple tap or click instead (2.5.1, 2.5.7).
- **No time limits** on forms or content. If a time limit is unavoidable, warn people and let them extend it (2.2.1).
- Forms have visible labels, give instructions before the field, explain errors in words, and do not ask for the same information twice (3.3.1, 3.3.2, 3.3.7). Do not make people solve puzzles or remember passwords to sign in (3.3.8).
- Put help in the same place on every page: our phone number and email address, as well as any online option (3.2.6).
- Link text says where the link goes, for example "Find a warm meal near you". Never use "click here" or "read more".

### Images

Every meaningful image needs alt text: a short description that screen readers read aloud (WCAG 1.1.1). Describe what matters for the purpose of the image, in about 125 characters or fewer. Do not start with "Image of" or "Photo of".

| Type | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Informative | What the image shows that matters | "Volunteers serve hot soup to families at the Harbourside community kitchen." |
| Functional (a link or button) | Where it goes or what it does | A phone icon linking to our helpline: "Call our helpline" |
| Decorative | Nothing. Use `alt=""` on the web and leave the alt field empty on social media | A wave pattern behind a heading |
| Complex (charts, maps, infographics) | A short summary, plus the full information in text or a table nearby | "Bar chart: meals served rose each month from January to June. Full figures in the table below." |
| Text in an image | The same words as the image | A poster that says "Free lunch, Saturday 12 till 2": write exactly that |

**Logo alt text:** "Harbourside Community Trust logo". When the logo links to the home page, use "Harbourside Community Trust home".

> **Do** describe people by what they are doing, not what they look like, unless their appearance is the point of the image.

> **Don't** put important information only in an image. Repeat it in the text or caption.

### Video and audio

- **Captions** on every video, checked by a person for accuracy and speaker names (1.2.2). Automatic captions are a starting point, not a finished job. For social media, burn captions into the video as well, because many people watch with the sound off.
- **Transcripts** for every audio clip, podcast and WhatsApp voice note we send to groups.
- **Audio description**, or a described transcript, when important information is shown but not said (1.2.5). The easiest fix is to say it out loud: "You'll see our address on screen: …".
- **No autoplay with sound.** Videos on our website do not play until someone presses play.
- Speak clearly, with background music kept low, so people with hearing loss can follow.

### Motion

- Respect the device's "reduce motion" setting. When it is on, replace movement with fades of 120ms or less, and stop parallax, auto-advancing carousels and looping animation. See Motion (chapter 10).
- Nothing flashes more than three times a second (2.3.1). This includes GIFs on social media.
- Anything that moves, scrolls or updates by itself for more than five seconds has a pause or stop control (2.2.2).

### Documents, PDFs and letters

Most of our documents are read by people using screen readers, magnifiers or printouts. Build accessibility in from the start, using the templates in Presentations and documents (chapter 17).

- Use the built-in **heading styles** (Heading 1, Heading 2) in order, real bulleted and numbered lists, and tables with a header row. Do not use empty lines or spaces to create layout.
- Set the document **title and language** (English (United Kingdom)) in the file properties.
- Add alt text to images and mark decorative images as decorative.
- Make the **reading order** logical: check it in the Word or PowerPoint selection pane.
- Run the **Accessibility Checker** in Microsoft Word or PowerPoint and fix every error before you share or export.
- Export **tagged PDFs** (in Word, "Save as PDF" with document structure tags turned on). Add bookmarks to anything over 10 pages. Check the PDF with PAC (PDF Accessibility Checker) or Adobe Acrobat's accessibility check.
- For information on the website, publish a web page rather than a PDF. If you must use a PDF, also offer the content in Word or as a web page.
- **Letters** use the body font at 14pt minimum, left-aligned, on white or cream paper, not glossy. Put the most important information, and our phone number, at the top. Offer large print, Easy Read, audio and other languages, and record people's format preferences so we use them every time.

> **Don't** scan a printed page and share it as a PDF. A scanned page is a picture, so screen readers cannot read the text.

### Social media and WhatsApp

The Social media chapter (chapter 15) has the full rules and its own accessibility section. The essentials:

- Add alt text to every image in the platform's alt text field on Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn.
- WhatsApp has no alt text field, so describe important images in the message text.
- Caption every video and burn the captions in.
- Write hashtags in CamelCase, for example #HarboursideTrust, and put no more than three at the end.
- Use emoji sparingly, at the end of a sentence, and never in place of words. Screen readers read out every emoji name.
- Never use "fancy" Unicode letters for bold or italic effects. Screen readers cannot read them.
- Put any words that appear in an image in the post text as well.

### Events and physical spaces

Our events must welcome older residents, disabled people and families with young children.

- **Before booking a venue**, check for step-free access to the entrance, main room and an accessible toilet. Check for a hearing loop, good lighting, seating with backs and arms, and accessible parking or a nearby bus stop.
- **In every invitation**, describe the access (for example, "Step-free entrance from the main car park. Hearing loop in the main hall"). Give a phone number and email address for asking for adjustments, and a date to ask by. Do not invite people by social media or online forms alone.
- **On request**, provide **British Sign Language (BSL)** interpreters and live captioning by a speech-to-text reporter (CART). Book them at least four weeks ahead, as they are in short supply.
- Have **large print** copies of anything handed out, and a printed agenda.
- Offer a **quiet space** away from the main room, and a clear way to leave.
- Speakers use a microphone every time, including for questions from the floor, so the hearing loop works.
- Signs use the brand's high-contrast pairings, large text and simple words, with symbols plus words.

### Testing and sign-off

Automated tools find only some problems. Test every new web page, template, campaign and important document in these ways.

1. **Automated:** run axe DevTools, WAVE or Lighthouse on web pages, and the Microsoft or PAC checker on documents.
2. **Keyboard:** use Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter and Space to reach and use everything. Focus must always be visible.
3. **Screen reader:** NVDA on Windows, VoiceOver on iPhone or Mac, and TalkBack on Android. Check headings, links, alt text, form labels and reading order.
4. **Zoom and reflow:** zoom to 200% and 400%, or use a 320px-wide screen. Nothing should be cut off or overlap.
5. **Colour:** check contrast for any new pairing, and view the design in greyscale to confirm nothing depends on colour.
6. **Plain English:** read the copy aloud, or run it through a readability checker, and aim for a reading age of 9 to 11.
7. **Real people:** test important services and campaigns with the people who will use them. Include older residents, disabled people and people who speak English as a second language. Pay them or offer vouchers for their time, and test both online and offline routes.

**Sign-off:** the person who made the work completes the checks. The Communications team gives final approval before anything is published. Anything that fails a contrast, alt text, caption or keyboard check is not published until it is fixed. *Proposed — confirm a named accessibility lead within the Communications team.*

> **Accessibility** If you are not sure whether something is accessible, ask before you publish, not after.

### Buttons and links

| Element | Style | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Primary button | `surface-brand` fill with `text-on-brand` label, `label` type token, radius `md` | One per view, for the main action ("Get help", "Volunteer", "Donate") |

### Forms

- Every field has a visible label above it in `label` type. Placeholder text is never the only label.
- Field borders use `border-strong` (at least 3:1 against the page). Radius `sm`.
- Mark required fields with the word "(required)", not only an asterisk or colour.
- Errors: show the `error-fg` text with an error icon directly under the field, and an error summary at the top of the form that links to each field. Keep brand Sunset Coral out of error messages so people do not confuse a campaign colour with a problem.
- Ask only what you need. Explain why you ask for sensitive details such as income or housing situation, in plain English.
- Allow enough time, never expire a form without warning, and let people go back without losing what they typed.

| State | Treatment |
|---|---|

### Post design

Templates for every format are in `assets/social/<platform>/`. Each format has an editable SVG with a `guides` layer, which you must hide before export, and a safe-zone overlay. Start from a template every time.

> **Warning** In this first version of the kit, the generated templates need three fixes before use. The supporting line on post templates is too small: set it to at least 4% of the image height (54px on a 1080 × 1350 post) in Atkinson Hyperlegible Next, and keep the headline short enough to stay inside the safe zone. On the Facebook cover, keep text at least 17% in from each side, because phones crop the edges. On the LinkedIn cover, remove the supporting line. Also note that the PNG previews centre the logo, but the SVG templates place it bottom-left. Follow the SVG templates.

#### Layout and the logo

- Place the full-colour or white logo in the **bottom-left corner, inside the safe zone**. It should be about a quarter of the image width and never less than 160px wide. On Stories, Reels and WhatsApp Status, keep it above the bottom safe zone, because the reply bar covers the bottom edge.
- On Reels, the bottom 35% is covered by captions and buttons. Keep all text and the logo above it.
- Use one logo per post. On a photo, use the white logo on a Harbour Blue band rather than straight on a busy image.

#### Text on images

- At most **20 words** on an image. If you need more, put it in the caption.
- Make text **at least 4% of the image height** (54px on a 1080 × 1350 post). Headlines use Nunito Bold or ExtraBold. Supporting text uses Atkinson Hyperlegible Next.
- Left-align text. Use sentence case, not capitals.
- Repeat every word from the image in the caption or alt text.

#### Colour on posts

The colour pairings below have been checked against our contrast rules.

| Use | Pairing | Contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Default post | White text on Harbour Blue (`primary`) | 8.4:1 |
| Light post | Harbour Blue text on white or `primary-100` | 8.4:1 / 7.1:1 |
| Warm highlight panel | White text on `secondary-600` | 5.8:1 |
| Sunset Coral panel | Near-black (`neutral-950`) text on Sunset Coral | 6.5:1 |

Sunset Coral and Tide Blue are for shapes, waves and decoration. Never use Sunset Coral text on Harbour Blue or on white, because both fail (2.84:1 and 2.95:1).

#### Carousels

Use the same cover design on every carousel. Number the slides ("2 of 5") in the bottom-right corner. End with one clear call to action, such as "Find your nearest food club: link in bio." Use no more than 10 slides.

#### Video

- Use a brand intro of no more than 2 seconds, or none at all, because people scroll past slow starts. Keep the outro end card to 5 seconds or less, with one call to action.
- **Lower thirds:** a Harbour Blue bar with the person's first name and role in white Atkinson Hyperlegible Next. Use a first name only, and only with consent.
- **Burned-in captions:** white Atkinson Hyperlegible Next on a near-black box (`neutral-950` at 80% or more opacity). Use at most 2 lines of about 40 characters, placed above the platform's bottom safe zone.

> **Do** start from the template, check the safe zone, then hide the `guides` layer before export.

> **Don't** stretch, recolour or add effects to the logo, or put text on a photo without a solid panel behind it.

### Writing for social

Follow chapter 11, Voice and tone: kind, plain and local. Social posts are casual. Replies to complaints are serious and kind.

- **Plain English for everyone.** Many readers speak English as a second language, and many are older. Use short sentences, common words and no idioms or slang. Write numbers as digits and dates in full ("Tuesday 4 November").
- **Structure:** start with a hook in the first 125 characters, because most platforms cut the post off there. Then make the point. End with one call to action, then any hashtags.
- **Length:** Facebook and LinkedIn posts are under 100 words. Instagram captions are under 150 words. WhatsApp messages are under 60 words, with the most important fact first.
- **Links:** say where a link goes ("Book a place on our website: [link]"). Never write "click here". Avoid unexplained short links.
- **Mentions and tagging:** tag partners only when they took part and have agreed. Never tag or name a person who receives our help.
- **Translated posts:** for safety-critical updates (closures, flooding, food club changes), publish translations as separate posts or messages. Start each one with the language name written in that language. Use a professional translator or a trained community volunteer to check every translation. Do not rely only on machine translation. Never put translated text only inside an image. *Proposed — confirm with frontline teams:* the main community languages to translate into.

### Hashtags

- Write every hashtag in **CamelCase** (`#HarboursideTrust`, not `#harboursidetrust`) so screen readers say the words correctly.
- Put hashtags **at the end** of the post, never in the middle of a sentence.
- Use **no more than 3 hashtags** per post on any platform. On Facebook use 0–1, on Instagram and LinkedIn 2–3, and on WhatsApp none.
- *Proposed brand hashtags:* `#HereForHarbourside` (our proposed tagline) and `#HarboursideTrust`. Check that nobody else is using them before launch.
- Before using a campaign or awareness-day hashtag, check what else it is being used for.

### Emoji

Use emoji to add warmth, not meaning. Our approved set is red heart (thanks), waving hand (welcome), house (homes), steaming bowl (meals), handshake (partners), calendar (dates) and sun (good news). Screen readers read each one aloud by name.

- Use **at most 2 emoji** per post, at the end of a sentence, never in place of a word.
- Never repeat an emoji in a row, and never put emoji in display names or handles.
- **No emoji** in crisis posts, safety updates, complaint replies or anything about hardship.

### Accessibility on social

This section is mandatory. A post that fails it is not ready to publish.

- **Alt text on every image.** Describe what the image shows and why it matters, in about 125 characters. For an infographic, use longer alt text and link to a text version. Never start with "Image of".
- **WhatsApp has no alt text.** Put all the essential information in the message text, never only in the image.
- **Captions on every video.** Check and correct auto-captions before publishing. Add a transcript for videos longer than 1 minute. Make sure videos make sense with the sound off.
- **Hashtags in CamelCase**, with emoji limited as above.
- **No Unicode "fonts".** Never use fake bold or italic letters from text-generator websites. Screen readers read them as symbols or skip them.
- **Text in images is repeated** in the caption or alt text.
- **Contrast:** at least 4.5:1 for text on graphics, or 3:1 for very large headlines. Use the pairings in the post design section.
- **No flashing** of more than 3 flashes a second. Avoid fast cuts and strobe effects.
- **Plain language:** spell out acronyms and make links descriptive.

### Safeguarding, consent and privacy

The people we help can be vulnerable. Protecting them matters more than any post.

- **Written consent is required** before posting any photo, video or story of a person. Use the Trust's photo consent form, and keep it linked to the image. For children under 18, a parent or guardian must sign. Consent can be withdrawn at any time. Remove the content within 2 working days if it is.
- **Never identify a person receiving help** without their written consent, by name, face, voice, home, school or anything else that could identify them. If you are unsure, use photos of hands, places or volunteers instead.
- **Never share the location of vulnerable people**, such as a home, a temporary housing address or a refuge. Turn off geotagging. Post event photos after people have left.
- **Never ask for personal details in public.** Move the conversation to a private message.

### WhatsApp: consent and privacy

- Use the **WhatsApp Channel** or **broadcast lists** for families. Use them instead of groups, because groups show every member's phone number to the others.
- Add people only after they **opt in**, and record when they did and how. Every broadcast message ends with: "Reply STOP to stop these messages." Remove anyone who replies STOP the same working day.
- Volunteer groups are allowed only with each member's agreement. Set them so that only admins can post, and include at least two staff admins.
- Never share personal details, case information or photos of service users on WhatsApp.

### Community management

We reply as a person from the Trust: kind, clear and brief. Sign replies with a first name if the team agrees to that.

| Situation | Response time (working hours, guidance) | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Someone asks for help in a comment or message | Within 1 working day | Reply kindly, move to a private message, and signpost to the right service. Never assess need on social media |
| Someone may be in immediate danger | As soon as seen | Tell them to call 999. Alert the duty manager and follow the safeguarding policy |
| General question | Within 1 working day | Answer, or link to the right web page |
| Complaint | Acknowledge within 4 working hours | Say sorry where we got it wrong, take it to a private message, and follow up publicly once it is resolved |
| Wrong information about us | Same working day | Correct it calmly, with a source |
| Abuse, hate, harassment, scams | As soon as seen | Hide or remove, report to the platform and log a screenshot. Do not reply |

- **Out of hours:** set an automatic reply on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp. It should give our opening hours and say: "If you or someone else is in danger, call 999."
- **Escalation:** the team member handles it first. If unsure, go to the Communications lead, then the chief executive. Safeguarding concerns go straight to the safeguarding lead.
- **Moderation rules:** pin the house rules in Facebook groups. We remove abuse, discrimination, personal information, spam and scams. We do not remove criticism that is polite.

### Crisis and sensitive moments

Storms, flooding, service closures, a data breach or negative press all need a fast, calm and accurate response.

1. **Pause** all scheduled posts on every channel straight away. Anyone on the team can do this.
2. **Confirm the facts** with the duty manager. Do not guess about causes or blame.
3. **Post a holding statement** within 1 hour if families are affected, after the Communications lead approves it. For example: "Our [service] at [place] is closed today because of [flooding]. Your nearest open point is [place, time]. We will update you here by [time]."
4. **Get approval:** the Communications lead approves service updates. The chief executive approves anything about safety, reputation or the media. For a data breach, the data protection lead decides whether to report it to the ICO, which must happen within 72 hours for breaches that have to be reported. Post nothing about a breach until the data protection lead and the chief executive approve it.
5. **Update** at the times you promised, on Facebook and WhatsApp first, in plain English and the agreed translations.
6. **Stay silent** about tragedies that have nothing to do with us. Do not post promotional or upbeat content during a local emergency.

### Staff and volunteer advocacy

We welcome staff and volunteers sharing our work.

- Share official posts, and add your own words in your own voice.
- Say you work or volunteer for us when you post about us. On LinkedIn, the personal banner template (in `assets/social/linkedin/`) is available for staff.
- Your personal account is yours. Do not speak for the Trust, reply to complaints or make promises about services.
- Never post photos, names or stories of people we help from a personal account. Never give advice about someone's case. Signpost them to our official channels.

### Fundraising appeals

Our appeals follow the Fundraising Regulator's Code of Fundraising Practice. Fundraising must be legal, open, honest and respectful.

- Say clearly what the money is for, and use only real, checked figures. When a figure shows only an example ("£10 could pay for…"), say so.
- Do not pressure people, use guilt, or target people who may be in vulnerable circumstances.
- Use real stories only with written consent. Never exaggerate need.
- The Communications lead must check every fundraising post.

### Partners, influencers and user-generated content

- **Disclosure:** if a business pays, gifts or rewards someone to post about us, the post must say so clearly at the start. Use `#Ad` or the platform's paid-partnership label, following the UK ASA/CAP advertising rules. Ask for a legal check if you are unsure.
- **Co-branded posts:** follow chapter 19, Co-branding and partnerships. Our logo and the partner's logo should be the same visual size, inside the safe zone.
- **Reposting user content:** ask the owner for permission in writing (a message is fine) and credit them by handle. Never repost an image showing a child or a person we help, even with the owner's permission, unless we also hold our own written consent.

## Print and stationery

Printed leaflets, letters and posters reach people who do not use the internet, including many older residents. Print must be easy to read in a dim community hall or on a doorstep, look warm and trustworthy, and reproduce our colours consistently from one printer to the next.

> **Warning** We only have the logo as a PNG. Before any professional print job, ask the Communications team for vector artwork (PDF, EPS or SVG). Until a designer supplies it, the PNG may be used only for office printing at the size it was supplied or smaller.

### Accessible print: our baseline

These rules apply to everything we print for the public:

- Body text at least 14pt in Atkinson Hyperlegible Next (or Verdana or Arial if the brand fonts are not installed). Captions, footers and legal lines at least 10pt; only business cards go smaller (see below).
- Offer a large-print version (16–18pt body) of every letter, leaflet and form on request, and say so on the item: "Need this in large print, another language or another format? Call [Phone number] or email comms@harbourside.example."
- Use dark text on white or a very light tint. Body text is `text-default` (neutral-900). Headings can be Harbour Blue.
- Never set text over a photo or pattern. Put text on a solid panel.
- Left-align text with a ragged right edge. No justified text, no long passages in capitals or italics.
- Print on matt or silk paper, never gloss, to cut glare for people with low vision.
- Keep at least 90gsm paper for letters so text does not show through.
- Pair every QR code with a short web address written out in full, and a phone number.

> **Do** use plain English and short sentences, with one idea per paragraph, and lists for steps.

> **Don't** print grey text on coloured paper, or print Sunset Coral or Tide Blue text. Use `secondary-600` if a coral heading is essential.

### Business cards

| Spec | Rule |
|---|---|
| Size | 85 × 55mm, 3mm bleed, 4mm safe margin |
| Stock | Matt, at least 350gsm, ideally recycled or FSC certified |
| Front | Full-colour logo on white, at least 40mm wide, top left |
| Back (optional) | Harbour Blue flood with the single-colour light symbol centred |
| Text | Name 11pt bold, role and contact details at least 9pt; legal text never below 8pt |

Information order: name, role, phone, email, website, then "[Registered charity number]".

### Letterhead and letters to families

- A4, margins at least 20mm. Logo top left, 50–60mm wide, with its clear space.
- Footer: "[Registered address]", "[Registered charity number]", phone, email and website in 10pt `text-muted`.
- Use the Word template (chapter 17) rather than pre-printed paper, so letters can be made in large print or translated without reprinting stock.
- Letters to families use 14pt body text, a clear heading saying what the letter is about, and a "What to do next" box near the top on `surface-subtle`.
- For translated letters, keep the layout and the logo the same and add an English line naming the language and how to get help.

### Envelopes and compliment slips

| Item | Size | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| DL envelope | 110 × 220mm | Logo top left on the back flap or front, 40mm wide; respect Royal Mail address and window zones |
| C5 envelope | 162 × 229mm | As DL |
| C4 envelope | 229 × 324mm | For reports and large-print packs |
| Compliment slip | 99 × 210mm | Logo top left at 40–50mm, address line at the foot |

Use a plain return address ("If undelivered, please return to [Registered address]"). Avoid messages on the outside that reveal a family's situation, such as "Food bank" or "Housing support", to protect their privacy.

### Leaflets, flyers and posters

- **Sizes:** A5 and DL for leaflets, A4 and A3 for posters in community venues.
- **Grid:** 6 columns for A4 and A3, 4 columns for A5. Margins at least 10mm on leaflets, 15mm on posters.
- **Bleed and safe area:** 3mm bleed on every edge, and keep text and the logo at least 5mm inside the trim.
- **Logo:** at least 40mm wide; top left or bottom centre, in the same place on every item in a campaign.
- **Hierarchy:** one headline (Nunito bold), one image, three or four short points, one call to action with a phone number. Posters must be readable from 2m: headlines at least 72pt on A3, body at least 24pt.
- **Colour:** follow the 60/25/10/5 proportions from chapter 04. A Harbour Blue or Sunset Coral band works well for headers; text on Sunset Coral must be neutral-950, never white.
- **Images:** 300ppi at final size, in CMYK, with photographs that follow chapter 08.

### Print colour and proofing

- Use the CMYK values in chapter 04 as a starting point only. They are mathematical conversions and must be checked with the printer.
- Pantone references are to be matched from a physical Pantone swatch book. Coated (C) and uncoated (U) papers give visibly different results, so choose a match for each stock you use.
- Always ask for a printed proof on the final paper for any new item or new printer. Check Harbour Blue does not drift towards purple and Sunset Coral does not turn orange.
- Use 100% black (K) for small text; ask the printer for their rich black only for large dark areas.
- Reversed text (white on Harbour Blue) must be at least 10pt and regular weight or heavier, so it does not fill in on uncoated paper.

### Working with printers

Send print-ready PDFs (PDF/X-4) with fonts embedded, 3mm bleed and crop marks. Ask the Communications team (comms@harbourside.example) to approve every proof before it goes to press, and allow at least 5 working days for approval and proofing.

- 16:9 at 1920 × 1080px. Keep text and logos inside a safe area 64px from every edge.
- Slide text at least 24pt; titles 36–44pt. If your words do not fit at 24pt, split the slide.
- One idea per slide, and no more than 40 words of body text.
- Fonts: Nunito for titles and Atkinson Hyperlegible Next for text. If they are not installed, the template falls back to Arial (titles) and Verdana (text). Do not substitute other fonts.
- Colour: white or `surface-subtle` backgrounds for content slides; Harbour Blue for section slides. Text on Sunset Coral is neutral-950, never white.
- Logo: on the title and end slides only, plus the small symbol bottom right of content slides at 24px or larger. Not on every corner of every slide.

> **Do** give every slide a unique title, even if you hide it visually, so people using a screen reader can navigate.

> **Don't** put text inside images or screenshots of tables. Recreate them as live text or a real table.

### Accessible slides

1. Use layout placeholders so the reading order is correct. Check it in the Selection Pane (PowerPoint) or by tabbing through (Google Slides).
2. Add alt text to every meaningful image, chart and icon. Mark decorative images as decorative.
3. Write link text that makes sense out of context ("Volunteer handbook (PDF, 2MB)").
4. Avoid animations and transitions other than a simple fade.
5. Run the built-in accessibility checker (PowerPoint: Review > Check Accessibility) before you share or present.
6. Share the slides before the session, in the original format and as a tagged PDF.

### Documents: use real styles

Screen readers, translation tools and large-print conversion all rely on styles. Never fake a heading by making text bigger or bold.

| Style | Font and size (Word) | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Nunito bold 28pt, Harbour Blue | Once, on the cover |
| Heading 1 | Nunito bold 20pt, Harbour Blue | Main sections |
| Heading 2 | Nunito bold 16pt, neutral-900 | Sub-sections |
| Heading 3 | Atkinson Hyperlegible Next bold 14pt | Minor headings |
| Normal | Atkinson Hyperlegible Next 12pt, line spacing 1.5 | Partner and internal reports |
| Normal (public) | Atkinson Hyperlegible Next 14pt, line spacing 1.5 | Letters and documents for families |
| Caption | Atkinson Hyperlegible Next 11pt, `text-muted` | Figure and table captions |

- Use Heading 1–3 in order with no skipped levels. Generate the contents from the headings.
- Use the built-in bulleted and numbered list styles.
- Tables: simple grids only, with a header row set to repeat on each page, no merged or empty cells used for layout.
- Left-align all text. No justified text and no text boxes for main content.
- Charts follow chapter 09 and have a short text summary beneath them.
- Set the document language (English (UK), or the translation's language) and the document title in File > Properties.

> **Accessibility** Reports for council partners are often required to meet public-sector accessibility rules. Build them accessibly from the start; fixing a PDF afterwards is slow and unreliable.

### Exporting to PDF

- Export from Word with "Document structure tags for accessibility" switched on, creating bookmarks from headings. In Google Docs, download as PDF and then check the tags; if they are missing, export via Word.
- The PDF must have a title, language set, tags, bookmarks for documents over 4 pages, and a logical reading order.
- Check every PDF with an accessibility checker (Adobe Acrobat's Accessibility Check, or the free PAC tool) before publishing.
- Always publish an accessible HTML page or Word version alongside a PDF on the website.
- For print, export a separate PDF/X-4 file following chapter 16.

### File naming and version control

- Name files: `harbourside-[type]-[short-title]-[yyyy-mm-dd].[ext]`, for example `harbourside-report-winter-food-hubs-2026-11-30.pdf`. Lowercase, hyphens, no spaces.
- Add `-large-print` or a language code (for example `-pl` or `-ar`) for alternative versions.
- Keep one master in the shared drive. Mark drafts with `-draft-v1`, `-draft-v2` and remove the suffix only when approved.
- Record the owner and review date on the last page of policies and reports.

> **Do** check a translated version with a fluent speaker from the community before it goes out.

> **Don't** email large attachments to families; send a short message with a link, and offer a printed copy.


## Signage, environment and merchandise

Signs, banners and volunteer clothing are how people find us in a church hall, a library or a busy street, and how they know who to ask for help. They must be easy to read from a distance and for people with sight loss, and make volunteers instantly recognisable without making anyone who uses our services feel singled out.

> **Warning** Signs, banners, clothing and printed merchandise all need vector logo artwork. We only have a PNG today. Ask the Communications team (comms@harbourside.example) for vector files before ordering; no supplier should redraw or trace the logo themselves.

### Signs at our hubs and venues

Most of our signs are temporary: A-boards, pull-up banners and laminated posters in venues we share. The same rules apply to permanent signs.

| Rule | Specification |
|---|---|
| Typeface | Atkinson Hyperlegible Next bold for messages, Nunito bold for headlines. Sentence case, never all capitals |
| Letter size | About 25mm cap height for every 10m of viewing distance; at least 15mm for directional signs read close up |
| Logo size | At least 300mm wide for every 10m of viewing distance, so "Harbourside" stays readable |
| Contrast | Dark text on white, or white on Harbour Blue. Aim for a light reflectance value (LRV) difference of at least 70 points between text and background |
| Finish | Matt or satin, never gloss, so lights and sunshine do not cause glare |
| Wording | Short and positive: "Food hub open today, 10am to 2pm. Everyone welcome." |

- Use the full-colour logo on white signs and the single-colour light logo on Harbour Blue signs.
- Put the key message, the opening times and a phone number on every hub sign. Add simple symbols for food, housing advice and work support, following the icon style in chapter 07.
- For permanent signs, follow current BS 8300 guidance and Approved Document M on contrast, mounting heights, tactile lettering and braille. Ask the venue owner, who is usually responsible for the building, before fixing anything.

> **Do** test a new sign by reading it from the far end of the room, with reading glasses off.

> **Don't** put signs on patterned walls or over posters. Mount them on a plain board with clear space around.

### Wayfinding and safety

- Use standard pictograms (ISO 7001) for toilets, accessible toilets, baby changing, lifts and information. Do not redraw them in a brand style.
- Fire, first-aid and safety signs follow BS ISO 7010 and the venue's own signs. Never rebrand or recolour safety signs.
- Use arrows and words together ("Food hub, first floor" with an arrow), never arrows alone.

### Glass doors and office branding

- Glass doors and screens need visible markings (manifestations) so people do not walk into them. In England, Approved Document K sets the heights; check the current edition. A row of Harbour Blue symbols, or a Harbour Blue band with the white symbol, works well. Do not use Tide Blue alone: it is too pale to be seen against a bright room behind the glass.
- Reception or entrance walls: the full-colour logo on a white or very light wall, at least 600mm wide, with the clear space from chapter 03. On a Harbour Blue wall, use the single-colour light logo.

### Events

| Item | Specification |
|---|---|
| Roll-up banner | 850 × 2000mm. Logo in the top 400mm, message in the top 1200mm. Nothing important in the bottom 800mm, which tables and people hide |
| Table cover | Harbour Blue with the single-colour light logo centred on the front drop |
| Gazebo or flag | Single-colour light logo on Harbour Blue |
| Name badge | A6 or 100 × 70mm. First name at least 24pt bold, role ("Volunteer", "Staff") at least 14pt |
| Collection tin or bucket label | Full-colour logo on white, the full name in text and "[Registered charity number]" |

- Add "I speak [Language]" badges for volunteers who speak community languages; they help families who speak English as a second language find someone to talk to.
- For street or house-to-house collections, check the council licence conditions before printing labels.

### Volunteer clothing and lanyards

| Item | Treatment |
|---|---|
| T-shirt or hoodie | Harbour Blue garment, single-colour light logo on the chest (at least 80mm wide) and "Volunteer" in large white type on the back |
| Hi-vis vest | Yellow or orange vest with the single-colour dark logo on the back, printed on a panel away from the reflective tape |
| Lanyard | Harbour Blue, 15–20mm wide, the white symbol repeated with "Harbourside Community Trust" as text, breakaway safety clasp |
| Apron (food hubs) | Harbour Blue or white, embroidered logo on the chest |

- Lanyards are too narrow for the full logo at its 40mm minimum width, so use the symbol (at least 8mm) and the name as text.
- **Embroidery:** the thin white waves fill in when stitched small. Embroider the full logo at least 70mm wide and the symbol at least 25mm across; ask the embroiderer for a sew-out sample first. Match thread colours to Harbour Blue, Sunset Coral and white from the embroiderer's thread chart, against a printed proof. *Proposed — confirm with a designer.*
- Check with the hi-vis supplier that printing does not affect the garment's safety certification.
- Single-colour logos use the knockout style, so the garment colour shows through the sun and waves. Only use them on solid-colour fabric, never on patterned or camouflage fabrics.

> **Do** keep clothing simple and the same for every volunteer, so families can spot who to ask.

> **Don't** use Sunset Coral garments with a white logo; white on Sunset Coral is too low in contrast.

### Merchandise and food parcels

- Choose useful, durable and sustainable items: cotton tote bags, reusable cups, pens made from recycled material. Avoid single-use plastic novelties.
- Print in one colour wherever possible: the single-colour light logo on Harbour Blue items, or the single-colour dark logo on light items. Full colour only on white.
- Respect the minimum sizes: the full logo at least 40mm wide, or the symbol at least 8mm with the name in text on very small items such as pens.
- **Food parcels and bags given to families:** keep branding discreet, such as a small symbol or a sticker inside. People should be able to carry their shopping home without advertising that they used a food bank.

> **Accessibility** Merchandise is never the only way we share important information. Phone numbers and opening times always appear on leaflets and the website too.

### Vehicles

If we use or borrow a van, apply the single-colour light logo on Harbour Blue panels, or full colour on a white vehicle, at least 600mm wide on each side, with the website address and phone number in text at least 75mm high. Get written agreement from the vehicle owner before applying any graphics.


## Co-branding and partnerships

Almost everything we do happens together: with the council, other charities, local businesses and funders. Showing those relationships clearly and fairly builds trust with families and funders alike, and it reflects our value of working together. These rules make sure every partner is credited correctly and our logo stays legible next to theirs.

### Types of relationship

| Relationship | Example | Whose logo leads | How we show it |
|---|---|---|---|
| We lead, partners support | A Harbourside food hub run with a local church | Ours | Our logo first and largest; partner logos in a "With support from" row |
| Partner leads, we support | A council housing scheme we deliver part of | The partner's | Their logo first; ours in their supporter row, following their guidelines |
| Equal partnership | A joint project with another local charity | Shared | Logos side by side at equal optical size with a divider or "in partnership with" |
| Funder | A grant from "[Funder name]" | Ours | "Funded by" line and the funder's logo, using the funder's required wording |
| Sponsor | A local business sponsoring an event | Ours | "Sponsored by" line and the sponsor's logo, smaller than ours |
| Supplier | A printer or caterer | Ours only | Suppliers do not appear on our materials and may not use our logo without written permission |

### Lock-up rules

- **Optical size:** for equal partners, match the logos by visual weight, not by width. Our wide logo usually needs to be slightly wider than a square partner logo to look the same size.
- **Divider:** separate logos with a thin vertical line in `border-subtle`, or the words "in partnership with" in `small` type. The divider is as tall as the taller logo.
- **Spacing:** apply our clear space (the height of the sun in our symbol) on every side of the whole lock-up, and the same distance between each logo and the divider.
- **Order:** the lead organisation is first (left, or top in a stacked row). For equal partners, the host of the material goes first.
- **Alignment:** align logos on their vertical centres in a row. In a supporter row, keep every logo the same height and spaced evenly.
- **Minimum size:** our logo never goes below 160px or 40mm wide in a lock-up. If a supporter row would force it smaller, use fewer logos or move the row.

### Backgrounds for shared lock-ups

- Put lock-ups on white whenever possible. Our full-colour logo is approved on white only.
- On Harbour Blue or other colours, use our single-colour light or dark logo and ask partners for their equivalent single-colour version.
- Never place partner logos on a busy photo. Use a solid white strip along the bottom of posters and leaflets for the logo row.

> **Do** ask each partner for their latest logo files and brand guidelines. Their rules apply to their logo, just as ours apply to ours.

> **Don't** recolour a partner's logo in Harbour Blue to make it "match", or put our logo inside a partner's shape or frame.

### Funding and supporter lines

Many funders and the council require exact acknowledgement wording and logo placement as a condition of the grant. Check the grant agreement before designing anything.

- Use simple, consistent wording in `small` or `body` type above the logo row: "Funded by [Funder name]", "With support from [Partner name]", "Delivered in partnership with [Partner name]".
- On web pages, write the funder's name as text as well as showing the logo.
- On small items such as social posts, a text credit ("Funded by [Funder name]") is acceptable where the funder allows it.

### Projects and sub-brands

We are one organisation with one logo. Projects such as "[Project name]" do not get their own logos or symbols, which would confuse families and dilute recognition.

- Name projects in plain words: "[Project name], a Harbourside Community Trust project".
- Set the project name in Nunito bold with the full Harbourside logo nearby, at its normal minimum size.
- If a partner-led project needs its own identity, the Communications team agrees it with the partner and records it in this guide.

### Approvals and lead times

| Item | Who approves | Allow |
|---|---|---|
| Any use of our logo by a partner, funder or sponsor | Communications team | 5 working days |
| Co-branded print, signage or banners | Communications team and each partner | 10 working days, plus the partner's own approval time |
| Press releases naming partners | Communications team and each partner | 3 working days |

Send requests to comms@harbourside.example with the draft, where it will be used and the deadline. We supply our logo files; partners must not copy them from our website.

### Co-branding accessibility
- Both our logo and partner logos keep at least 3:1 contrast against the background behind them.
- Alt text names every organisation: "Logos of Harbourside Community Trust and [Partner name]". For a funder row: "Funded by [Funder name]".
- Never make the logo row the only place a partner or funder is credited in a document. Include them in the text too, so screen-reader users and anyone reading a plain-text version get the same information.
